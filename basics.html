The last samurai

Most of them were not born samurai.
They became the last samurai anyway.

Farmers’ sons and masterless swordsmen, the Shinsengumi chose their side in 1863 and kept to it until there was nothing left to keep to.

When their lord gave up Edo without a fight, they fought on. When their commander was executed, they fought on. Their vice-commander, Hijikata Toshizō, rode out to his death at Hakodate a week before the war ended, still serving the cause he had chosen six years before.

What made them samurai was not their birth. It was how they kept faith with the side they had chosen, literally to the end.

The rest of this site assumes you know their story. This page gives you what Japanese readers grow up with: the country they lived in, who fought whom, what the corps was, the people and the words you’ll meet, and how to read the names and dates. It takes about ten minutes.

01At a glance

What
A special police force that kept order in Kyoto, the emperor’s city, for the Tokugawa shogunate.
When
1863 to 1869. Five years as police in Kyoto, then a year and a half of civil war.
Who
Mostly not born samurai. Its leaders, Kondō Isami and Hijikata Toshizō, were farmers’ sons from Tama, just west of Edo (today’s Tokyo).
Size
Two dozen men at the start; some two hundred at its height.
Side
The losing one. They served the shogun, and the shogunate fell in 1868.
Why remembered
Loyalty to a lost cause, a self-made rise in a society fixed by birth, a harsh code, and some of the most dramatic fights of the age.

The name means the “newly selected corps”. Their pale blue jackets with a white zigzag at the cuffs, and the single character 誠 (makoto, sincerity) on their banner, are still instantly recognised in Japan. The zigzag strip under this page’s header is that pattern.

02Japan in the 1860s

Two centres of authority. For more than 250 years Japan had been run by the Tokugawa family. Its head, the shogun, ruled from Edo with his government, the shogunate (bakufu). The emperor lived in Kyoto, in the old capital, with great prestige but no real power: in theory the shogun governed on his behalf.

A patchwork of domains. Much of the country was divided into some 260 domains (han), each ruled by a lord (daimyō) with his own samurai, finances and army. Some large domains far from Edo, above all Satsuma in the south-west and Chōshū at the western tip of Honshu, had old reasons to resent the Tokugawa.

A fixed society. People were born into a status: samurai, farmer, artisan, merchant. Only samurai could carry two swords and hold office. That is why it mattered so much that the Shinsengumi’s leaders were farmers who made themselves samurai.

The crisis. In 1853 an American squadron under Commodore Perry sailed into Edo Bay and forced the country, closed to most foreigners for two centuries, to open. The shogunate gave way, and lost face. A movement grew under the slogan sonnō jōi, “revere the emperor, expel the barbarians”. Young radicals, called shishi (“men of high purpose”), began to assassinate officials and anyone seen as friendly to the West.

Kyoto becomes the battleground. The radicals gathered in Kyoto, around the emperor, and the city filled with political killings. In 1863 the shogunate put the lord of the Aizu domain, Matsudaira Katamori, in charge of the city’s security as Protector of Kyoto. The Shinsengumi became his special police.

03Who fought whom

It helps to know the two sides, with one warning: they shifted over the years, and almost everyone on both sides said they were serving the emperor.

The shogunate’s side
  • The shogunate — the Tokugawa government in Edo; the last shogun was Tokugawa Yoshinobu.
  • Aizu — a northern domain, fiercely loyal to the Tokugawa. Its lord was Protector of Kyoto and the Shinsengumi’s patron.
  • The Shinsengumi, and other shogunal police such as the Mimawarigumi.
Against the shogunate
  • Chōshū — the most radical domain; driven out of Kyoto in 1863 and the corps’ main enemy.
  • Satsuma — a powerful southern domain. It sided with Aizu against Chōshū in 1863–64, then secretly allied with Chōshū in 1866.
  • Shishi from many domains, including Tosa, such as Sakamoto Ryōma.

In 1867 the shogun handed his powers back to the emperor, and weeks later Satsuma and its allies declared the shogunate abolished. The Boshin War (1868–69) followed. The anti-shogunate side fought under the emperor’s banner and won; their new government is known as the Meiji government, after the young emperor’s reign name, and the change of regime as the Meiji Restoration. The Shinsengumi, once the shogunate’s police, were now officially rebels.

04The corps itself

Origins. In 1863 the shogunate hired some 230 masterless swordsmen (rōnin) to guard the shogun on a visit to Kyoto. Almost at once the force was ordered home. Two groups refused and stayed: the men of Kondō Isami’s small sword school in Edo, the Shieikan, and a group from Mito around Serizawa Kamo. Aizu took them on. They lodged with farming families in the village of Mibu, on the edge of Kyoto, which is why people called them the “wolves of Mibu”.

Work. Patrolling the streets, guarding officials, and hunting down the radicals who plotted in the city’s inns. Their most famous action, the raid on the Ikedaya inn in 1864, made them known across Japan.

Ranks. At the top were the commander (kyokuchō), Kondō Isami, and the vice-commander (fukuchō), Hijikata Toshizō. Below them came a general secretary, numbered units under captains (kumichō), and inspectors who handled intelligence and discipline. In 1867 the whole corps was made direct retainers of the shogun: legally, at last, samurai.

The code. The corps lived under strict rules, the kyokuchū hatto. As later accounts give them, they banned straying from the way of the samurai, leaving the corps, raising money privately, taking up lawsuits privately, and private fights, on pain of seppuku (ritual suicide). By most counts, more members died by the code or at their comrades’ hands than in battle with the enemy; even a senior founder, Yamanami Keisuke, was made to die by it.

05Who’s who

Japanese names put the family name first: Kondō is the family name, Isami the given name. On this site, people are usually called by their family name. Tap a name to read his life.

  • Kondō Isami 近藤勇Commander. Master of the Shieikan; the face and host of the corps. Executed by the new government in 1868.
  • Hijikata Toshizō 土方歳三Vice-commander, the “demon vice-commander” who built the corps and enforced its code. Fought on to Hakodate and died there in 1869. The site’s central figure.
  • Okita Sōji 沖田総司Captain of the 1st unit; the brilliant young swordsman who grew up in Kondō’s house. Died of tuberculosis in 1868.
  • Saitō Hajime 斎藤一Captain of the 3rd unit. Stayed to fight for Aizu, and later lived as a Tokyo policeman under another name.
  • Nagakura Shinpachi 永倉新八Captain of the 2nd unit. Survived, and his memoirs are the source of many famous scenes.
  • Yamanami Keisuke 山南敬助General secretary; scholarly and well liked. Ran away in 1865 and was made to commit seppuku.
  • Serizawa Kamo 芹沢鴨The corps’ first leader alongside Kondō. Violent and extortionate; killed by his own comrades in 1863.
  • Itō Kashitarō 伊東甲子太郎A scholar-swordsman who joined in 1864 and left with his followers in 1867. Killed by the corps at Aburanokōji.
  • Matsudaira Katamori 松平容保Lord of Aizu and Protector of Kyoto: the corps’ patron.

Also in the story: Tokugawa Yoshinobu, the last shogun; Emperor Kōmei, who died in 1867; Sakamoto Ryōma, the Tosa activist who helped bring Satsuma and Chōshū together, assassinated in 1867. All members →

06The story in twelve dates

  1. 1853Perry’s ships arrive. The shogunate’s authority begins to crack.
  2. 1863The Shieikan men go to Kyoto, stay on under Aizu, and take the name Shinsengumi. Serizawa is killed by his comrades.
  3. 1864The Ikedaya raid makes the corps famous. Weeks later Chōshū attacks Kyoto and is beaten at the palace gates.
  4. 1865Yamanami dies by the code. The corps moves to Nishi Honganji temple.
  5. 1866Satsuma and Chōshū secretly ally.
  6. 1867The corps become the shogun’s own retainers. The shogun returns power to the emperor. Itō is killed at Aburanokōji.
  7. Jan 1868The shogunate is abolished; the Boshin War begins at Toba–Fushimi. The corps is beaten by modern rifles and artillery.
  8. Spring 1868Defeat at Katsunuma. Edo surrenders without a fight. Kondō gives himself up at Nagareyama and is executed.
  9. Summer 1868Okita dies in Edo. Hijikata and Saitō fight in the north.
  10. Autumn 1868Aizu falls. Saitō stays; Hijikata sails north with the shogunate’s fleet to Ezo (Hokkaido).
  11. 1869Hijikata is killed at Hakodate. A week later the last holdouts surrender and the war is over.
  12. 1913Nagakura’s recollections are published in a Hokkaido newspaper, and the corps’ story begins to reach the public.

07Words you’ll meet

Bakumatsu 幕末
“The end of the shogunate”: the years from 1853 to 1868, the setting of this story.
Shogun / shogunate 将軍・幕府
The military ruler of Japan, and his government (bakufu), based in Edo.
Domain 藩
Han: a territory ruled by a lord with his own samurai, such as Aizu, Satsuma or Chōshū.
Daimyō 大名
The lord of a domain.
Samurai 侍・武士
The hereditary warrior class, the only people allowed to wear two swords and hold office.
Rōnin 浪人・浪士
A masterless samurai, or any swordsman without a lord. The corps began as rōshi, hired rōnin.
Retainer (of the shogun) 幕臣・直参
A samurai in the shogun’s direct service. The corps reached this rank in 1867.
Shishi 志士
“Men of high purpose”: the radical activists of the imperial cause, and the corps’ main targets.
Sonnō jōi 尊王攘夷
“Revere the emperor, expel the barbarians”: the radicals’ slogan.
Protector of Kyoto 京都守護職
The post created in 1863 to keep order in Kyoto, held by the lord of Aizu.
Mibu 壬生
The village on the edge of Kyoto where the corps first lodged.
Shieikan 試衛館
Kondō’s small fencing school in Edo, where the core members first gathered.
Tennen Rishin-ryū 天然理心流
The rough, practical school of swordsmanship that Kondō, Hijikata and Okita trained in.
Haori 羽織
A short jacket worn over kimono. The corps’ were pale blue with a white zigzag (dandara).
Makoto 誠
Sincerity: the single character on the corps’ banner.
Kyokuchū hatto 局中法度
The corps’ code of rules.
Seppuku 切腹
Ritual suicide by cutting the belly; a samurai’s death, and the penalty for breaking the code.
Boshin War 戊辰戦争
The civil war of 1868–69 between the new imperial government and the shogunate’s supporters.
Meiji Restoration 明治維新
The fall of the shogunate and the return of rule to the emperor, from which modern Japan grew.
Ezo 蝦夷地
The old name for Hokkaido, where the war ended at Hakodate.

08Reading this site

Names. Family name first, as in Japanese. The line over a vowel, as in Kondō, means the sound is held long: say it “Kon-doh”. People sometimes changed names, too: Saitō Hajime later lived as Fujita Gorō, and Kondō used false names at the end.

Dates. Japan used a lunar calendar until 1873. The map gives dates in our calendar, so the Ikedaya raid is July 8, 1864. The story chapters follow the record and use the old calendar, so the same night is “the 5th day of the 6th month”. The old months run roughly a month behind ours, and the old year began in late January or February.

Ages. Japanese sources often count age the old way: one at birth, and a year older every New Year. Where it matters, the site gives both, so Kondō died at thirty-three, or thirty-five by the old count.

History and legend. Much of what is “known” about the corps comes from memoirs written decades later, and from novels. The site keeps them apart. On the map, boxes headed Legend & disputed accounts mark stories that are told but not proven. In Experience the story, every line is labelled Record, Anecdote (a well-known story where the record is silent) or Context.

09How they are remembered

For decades after 1868 the official story was the winners’. The Shinsengumi were remembered as the killers of the men who made modern Japan, and the survivors mostly kept quiet. Families and friends in Tama kept their memory anyway, and in 1888 raised a monument to Kondō and Hijikata near Hijikata’s home village.

The tide turned in the twentieth century. Nagakura’s recollections appeared in 1913, Shimozawa Kan collected survivors’ accounts in the 1920s, and in the 1960s Shiba Ryōtarō’s novels, above all Moeyo Ken (“Burn, O Sword”) about Hijikata, made them heroes to a new generation. Since then they have filled television dramas, films, manga, anime and games, and each spring Hino, Hijikata’s home town, holds a Shinsengumi festival.

Part of the appeal is the paradox. They were a police force on the losing side, with assassinations and purges on their record. They were also outsiders who rose by their own effort, and who kept faith with their cause after their own lord had given up. The rest of this site tells that story, and tries to keep what happened apart from what has been told.
