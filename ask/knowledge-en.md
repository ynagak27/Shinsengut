# The Road of Shinsengumi — site content

Everything below is the website's own text. Event ids are in [ev:id], people ids in [p:id]. "Legend" lines are disputed or later traditions, not documented fact.

## Events (in date order)

### Serizawa Kamo is born [ev:serizawabirth]
When: c. 1826 · Where: Serizawa village, Hitachi — now Namegata, Ibaraki
People: Serizawa Kamo [p:serizawa] — Born into a gōshi family; even his birth year is uncertain.
The future Serizawa Kamo is born, by most accounts a younger son of a family of country samurai in Serizawa village, near Lake Kasumigaura in the Mito domain's sphere. The family took its name from the village and claimed descent from the district's medieval warriors. He grows up in the Mito world of revere-the-emperor scholarship and sword training that will produce the assassins of the Sakurada Gate — and him.
Legend & disputed: His birth year, original name and place in the family are all disputed.

### Inoue Genzaburō is born [ev:inouebirth]
When: 1829 · Where: Hino, Musashi — now Hino, Tokyo
People: Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — Born a younger son of a Thousand-Man Guard family.
Inoue Genzaburō is born in Hino, the post town on the Kōshū road, into a family of the Hachiōji Thousand-Man Guard — farmers who held minor shogunal rank, kept their swords and took turns guarding the Tokugawa shrines at Nikkō. A younger son with no claim on the family's place, he will spend more than ten years earning his licence in the Tennen Rishin-ryū, the steady elder of a school full of prodigies. His descendants still keep his memory in a small museum near the old post town.

### Kiyokawa Hachirō is born [ev:kiyokawabirth]
When: November 24, 1830 · Where: Kiyokawa village, Shōnai — now Shōnai, Yamagata
People: Kiyokawa Hachirō [p:kiyokawa] — Born Saitō Motoji to a sake-brewing family.
The man who will set the Shinsengumi's story in motion is born Saitō Motoji, the son of a wealthy sake-brewing family in the village of Kiyokawa on the Mogami river, in the Shōnai domain. He will take the village's name as his own. Restless and brilliant, he goes to Edo to study, masters both Confucian learning and the sword, and becomes one of the most daring loyalist agitators of his generation — the man who talks the shogunate into raising the Rōshigumi. A memorial museum stands in the village today.

### Yamanami Keisuke is born [ev:yamanamibirth]
When: 1833 (traditional) · Where: Sendai — by tradition
People: Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami] — Born, by tradition, into a Sendai retainer family.
By tradition Yamanami Keisuke is born in 1833 into a family of retainers of the Sendai domain, the great house of the Date in the north. Almost nothing about his early life is documented. He will make his way to Edo, train in the Chiba family's Hokushin Ittō-ryū and become a man of learning as well as the sword, before attaching himself, by one story after losing a bout, to Kondō Isami's Shieikan.
Legend & disputed: His Sendai origin and birth year rest on tradition; other places have been suggested.

### Kondō Isami is born [ev:kondobirth]
When: October 9, 1834 · Where: Kami-ishihara village, Musashi — now Chōfu, Tokyo
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Born Miyagawa Katsugorō, third son of the house.
Miyagawa Katsugorō, the future Kondō Isami, is born the third son of a well-off farming family in Kami-ishihara, a village on the Kōshū road west of Edo. His father, Miyagawa Hisajirō, is a patron of the Tennen Rishin-ryū and keeps a practice hall in the house, so the boy grows up with the school's masters coming and going.
Like Hijikata's Ishida village a few kilometres away, Kami-ishihara lies in the Tama country — land ruled directly by the shogunate, where prosperous farmers read, write, keep swords and think of themselves as the Tokugawa's own people. Nothing about the birth marks it out. But the household's love of the sword, and its ties to the school, will decide the boy's life.

### Hijikata Toshizō is born [ev:birth]
When: May 31, 1835 · Where: Ishida village, Musashi — now Hino, Tokyo
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata]
Hijikata Toshizō is born the tenth and last child of a wealthy farming family on the Tama plain, west of Edo. His father dies before his birth, his mother when he is five. The Tama region is peculiar ground: legally farmland, but administered directly by the shogunate, its prosperous farmers proudly literate, sword-trained, and loyal to the Tokugawa house. Out of this soil — peasant status, samurai self-image — the future vice-commander grows. As a young man he peddles the family medicine, Ishida sanyaku, with a shinai strapped to his pack.
His sister Nobu married Satō Hikogorō, the headman of Hino, a patron of the Tennen Rishin-ryū who kept a practice hall in his own house; the school's masters came out from Edo to teach there, and it is there that Hijikata came to know Kondō Isami. Tradition says he was sent to Edo as a shop apprentice as a boy and came home after a quarrel. He also wrote haiku, and before leaving for Kyoto he gathered his poems into a small book under the pen name Hōgyoku.

### Itō Kashitarō is born [ev:itobirth]
When: 1835 · Where: Shizuku, Hitachi — now Kasumigaura, Ibaraki
People: Itō Kashitarō [p:ito] — Born Suzuki Ōkura, son of a minor retainer.; Suzuki Mikisaburō [p:suzuki] — Born here two years later, in 1837.
Itō Kashitarō is born Suzuki Ōkura in Shizuku, Hitachi province, the son of a minor retainer whose family soon falls on hard times. Hitachi lies next to Mito, the heartland of reverence for the emperor, and the boy absorbs its learning along with its swordsmanship. In Edo he will be adopted into the family of a Hokushin Ittō-ryū master and inherit his dōjō; his younger brother, Suzuki Mikisaburō, will follow him there and, later, into the Shinsengumi.
Legend & disputed: The map point is approximate: the exact site is not known or no longer exists.

### Nagakura Shinpachi is born [ev:nagakurabirth]
When: May 23, 1839 · Where: Matsumae domain residence, Shitaya, Edo
People: Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Born the son of a Matsumae retainer with a 150-koku stipend.
Nagakura Shinpachi is born in the Edo residence of the Matsumae domain, the small northern house that guarded the strait to Ezo, the son of a retainer with a respectable stipend. He is a samurai by birth, unlike most of Kondō's circle, and will give that up to live for the sword. The domain he leaves will take him back after the war — and, forty years later, it is to the far north, to Hokkaido, that he will retire.
Legend & disputed: The map point is approximate: the exact site is not known or no longer exists.

### Harada Sanosuke is born [ev:haradabirth]
When: 1840 · Where: Matsuyama, Iyo province
People: Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Born in Matsuyama; later a chūgen of the domain.
Harada Sanosuke is born in the castle town of Matsuyama on Shikoku, seat of a branch of the Tokugawa's own Matsudaira kin. He grows up to serve the domain as a chūgen, a servant below samurai rank — and, by his own oft-told story, answers a samurai's sneer that he did not know how to cut his belly by slashing it open on the spot. He survives, keeps the scar, and leaves Matsuyama to make his name with the spear.
Legend & disputed: The seppuku story comes from Harada himself and later retellings; the exact year and place are uncertain.

### Okita Sōji is born [ev:okitabirth]
When: 1842 or 1844 · Where: Shirakawa domain residence, Azabu, Edo
People: Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Born the son of a low-ranking Shirakawa retainer.
Okita Sōji is born in the Edo residence of the Shirakawa domain, where his father serves as a low-ranking retainer. The father dies when the boy is small; the family's place passes to his elder sister Mitsu's husband, Okita Rintarō, and at about nine Sōji is sent to live as a pupil in Kondō Shūsuke's Shieikan. The family temple, Senshō-ji, stands a few streets away — and it is there, some twenty-five years later, that he will be buried.
Legend & disputed: His birth year is given as both 1842 and 1844, and the site of the domain residence is approximate.

### Hijikata enters the Tennen Rishin-ryū [ev:shieikan]
When: 1859 · Where: Shieikan dojo, Ichigaya, Edo
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata]; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Master of the house — a farmer's son turned school head, hungry for a stage worthy of his sword.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — The live-in prodigy, already effectively head instructor while still in his teens.; Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — A senior pupil of more than ten years' standing and a fellow Hino man — the steady elder of the house.; Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami] — The educated Hokushin Ittō-ryū man, lending the rough house some polish.; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — A dojo-guest from the Matsumae clan who found the food bad and the company excellent.; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — The spearman of the circle — an outsider by style and temperament who fit anyway.; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo] — The youngest of the circle, a Hokushin Ittō-ryū man who had drifted into Kondō's company.
Hijikata formally enters the Tennen Rishin-ryū — a rough, practical rural school scorned by Edo's fashionable dojos as "potato-samurai" fencing. The Shieikan in Ichigaya is less an institution than a household: Kondō presiding, the teenage prodigy Okita as live-in disciple, the scholarly Yamanami and professional dojo-guests like Nagakura and Harada around the dinner table. It is poor, tight-knit, and ambitious. When the shogunate calls for swordsmen in 1863, this household will answer as one.
Kondō, a farmer's son from Kami-ishihara, had been adopted by the school's third master, Kondō Shūsuke. In 1861 he was formally installed as fourth master, celebrated with a mock battle between two teams of the school's men at the Rokusho shrine in Fuchū. The school now had a young head, a loyal following in the villages of Tama, and very little money. Two years later the shogunate's call for swordsmen offered something none of them had: a career.

### Kondō becomes fourth master [ev:succession]
When: October 1, 1861 · Where: Rokusho shrine, Fuchū — now Ōkunitama shrine
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Installed as fourth master of the school.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Took part in the mock battle.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Took part in the mock battle, still in his teens.; Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami] — Took part in the mock battle.; Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — Took part in the mock battle.
To mark Kondō Isami's installation as fourth master of the Tennen Rishin-ryū, the school stages a mock battle in the grounds of the Rokusho shrine in Fuchū, the old provincial capital of Musashi. The students are divided into two armies, red and white, each with its commander, and fight a field battle with practice weapons before the families and patrons of the Tama villages.
It is the school's great public day, and the names on its roster are the future Shinsengumi: Hijikata Toshizō, Okita Sōji, Yamanami Keisuke and Inoue Genzaburō among them. Two years later the same men will march west together.
Legend & disputed: The roster of the mock battle survives, but the roles given to particular men in it vary between later retellings.

### The Rōshigumi marches west [ev:roshigumi]
When: March 26, 1863 · Where: Denzū-in temple, Koishikawa, Edo
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — An unranked recruit in Kondō's group: a farmer's son with a sword licence and no title.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Given a modest post arranging lodgings ahead of the column — a long way from command.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Not yet twenty, and already the best swordsman of Kondō's house.; Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami]; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura]; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada]; Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue]; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo]; Serizawa Kamo [p:serizawa] — Already a leader of his own group; at Honjō, by Nagakura's later account, he lit a bonfire in the street when no room had been found for him.; Niimi Nishiki [p:niimi] — Marched west in Serizawa's Mito group.; Kiyokawa Hachirō [p:kiyokawa] — The scheme's author, travelling with the column and keeping his real plan to himself.
The shogun is to visit Kyoto, the first such journey in more than two centuries, and the city is full of loyalist swordsmen. Kiyokawa Hachirō's answer has been accepted: pardon the country's masterless fighters and hire them as a guard. Some 230 men gather at Denzū-in, a Tokugawa family temple in Koishikawa, and set out along the Nakasendō, the inland highway, for the fifteen-day march to Kyoto.
The recruits are a ragged mix of rōnin, farmers' sons, gamblers and genuine fencing masters. Kondō Isami's Shieikan household signs on together — Hijikata, Okita, Yamanami, Nagakura, Harada, Inoue, Tōdō — with no rank among them beyond a minor place in the column. So does a group from Mito around Serizawa Kamo, with a pedigree and a temper to match. Neither group yet knows that the man who organised the march means to hand it to the emperor's cause the moment it arrives.

### Arrival at Mibu [ev:mibu]
When: April 1863 · Where: Mibu village, Kyoto
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Begins organizing the corps' internal order — the work that will define him.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Junior commander, quietly building his own faction's credit with Aizu.; Okita Sōji [p:okita]; Saitō Hajime [p:saito]; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura]; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada]; Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami]; Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — Stays in Kyoto with Kondō's group and becomes an assistant vice-commander (副長助勤).; Serizawa Kamo [p:serizawa] — Senior commander by birth and pedigree — and already a walking diplomatic incident.; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo] — Stays in Kyoto with Kondō's group; at nineteen, the youngest of the founding core.; Kiyokawa Hachirō [p:kiyokawa] — Organiser of the Rōshigumi; at Mibu he reveals that he means to serve the emperor, not the shogunate, and leads most of the men back east.; Niimi Nishiki [p:niimi] — One of the corps' three first commanders, with Serizawa and Kondō.; Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — As Protector of Kyoto, takes the men who stayed behind under Aizu's authority.
The shogunate, desperate to police a Kyoto seething with loyalist assassins, recruits masterless swordsmen into the Rōshigumi and marches them west. The scheme immediately unravels — its organizer intends to turn the men to the loyalist cause — and the militia is ordered home. Two factions refuse: Serizawa's Mito men and Kondō's Shieikan household. Petitioning the Aizu domain, the protector of Kyoto, they are taken on as the Mibu Rōshigumi: two dozen unemployed swordsmen billeted on the farming village of Mibu, mocked in town as the "wolves of Mibu." It is a shabby beginning to a black-and-white legend.
The men are billeted on two leading households of Mibu, the Yagi and the Maekawa, whose houses still stand. They have a uniform made: a pale blue haori with white saw-tooth stripes at the cuffs, borrowed from the costume of the Forty-Seven Rōnin on the stage — the pattern that runs along the top of this page. After the corps' service in the August 1863 coup, they receive a new name: the Shinsengumi, the "newly selected corps."

### Kiyokawa Hachirō assassinated [ev:kiyokawadeath]
When: May 30, 1863 · Where: Ichinohashi bridge, Azabu, Edo
People: Kiyokawa Hachirō [p:kiyokawa] — Killed by shogunal swordsmen, weeks after turning the Rōshigumi against the regime that had paid for it.
Back in Edo with the Rōshigumi men who followed him home, Kiyokawa Hachirō is still scheming — now, it is feared, for a rising against the foreigners in Yokohama. The shogunate, which paid for his corps and was then betrayed by it, decides he must be stopped. Returning from a visit in Azabu, he is cut down near the Ichinohashi bridge by a group of shogunal swordsmen. He is thirty-two.
His death settles the shape of what he started. The men he led back east are reorganised as the Shinchōgumi, a police force in Edo under the Shōnai domain. The handful who refused to follow him are in Kyoto, under Aizu, and about to become the Shinsengumi. The two halves of the Rōshigumi will spend the rest of the decade serving the same shogunate, three hundred miles apart.
Legend & disputed: The killers are generally named as Sasaki Tadasaburō and his companions — the same Sasaki who, by later evidence, led the Mimawarigumi squad that killed Sakamoto Ryōma in 1867.

### Brawl with the Osaka sumo wrestlers [ev:osakasumo]
When: August 1863 · Where: Kitashinchi, Osaka
People: Serizawa Kamo [p:serizawa] — Struck down the wrestler on the bridge and led the party in the fight that followed.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — In the party that fought off the wrestlers.; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — In the party, and the main source for the night — his memoir tells the story in detail.; Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami] — In the party — an unlikely brawler, which says something about how the corps worked in its first year.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — In the party; by Nagakura's account it was Saitō's feeling unwell that brought them ashore.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Named in the party in Nagakura's account.
On duty in Osaka, a party of the corps led by Serizawa takes a boat out on the river to cool off, then heads into the pleasure quarter of Kitashinchi. On a narrow bridge a sumo wrestler refuses to give way, and Serizawa strikes him down. That night a crowd of wrestlers armed with staves comes to the teahouse where the swordsmen are drinking. The swordsmen draw. Wrestlers are cut down, at least one fatally, and the rest scatter.
The Osaka authorities are furious, and the corps' standing hangs on the outcome. In the end the sumo stables accept a settlement, and the incident is smoothed over with a charity sumo tournament in Kyoto under the corps' sponsorship. To the corps' Aizu patrons, though, it is one more entry in a growing list of Serizawa's outrages, and the list is nearly long enough.
Legend & disputed: Almost everything known about the night — who was there, who struck first, how many wrestlers died — comes from Nagakura's recollections decades later. The settlement and the charity tournament are better attested than the details of the fight.

### Purge of Serizawa Kamo [ev:serizawa]
When: October 1863 — exact date disputed · Where: Yagi residence, Mibu
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Widely held to have planned and led the assassination party.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Named among the assassins in most accounts.; Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami] — Named among the assassins in several accounts — the gentle man's darkest night.; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Named among the assassins in several accounts.; Serizawa Kamo [p:serizawa] — Killed in his sleep beside Oume after an evening of heavy drinking.; Niimi Nishiki [p:niimi] — Forced to commit seppuku in a Gion teahouse a few days before Serizawa was killed.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Commander when the decision was taken; not usually named among the men who did the killing, but the gainer by it.
After months of extortion, arson, and brawls that mortify the corps' Aizu patrons, the sentence comes down. On a night of driving rain, after a banquet at the Sumiya, assassins enter the Yagi house where Serizawa sleeps beside his mistress Oume. Both are killed; his lieutenant Hirayama dies in the next room. The corps blames Chōshū agents and stages a magnificent funeral. Everyone in the barracks understands. Command is now Kondō's alone, and the instrument of the purge — Hijikata's faction — becomes the corps' true spine. The Yagi family's descendants would later show visitors the sword scars in the doorframe.
Legend & disputed: Participant lists vary across sources (some add Tōdō Heisuke or Inoue Genzaburō, some drop Yamanami), and even the date differs between accounts. The rain, the party at the Sumiya, and the survival of the Yagi children hiding under their bedding come mainly from the Yagi family's later reminiscences.

### The Ikedaya Incident [ev:ikedaya]
When: July 8, 1864 · Where: Ikedaya inn, Sanjō-Kiyamachi, Kyoto
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Led the assault party through the front door with only a handful of men, fighting in the melee himself; his prized "Kotetsu" survived intact, as he proudly wrote home.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Cut down several men in the opening melee, then collapsed mid-fight and was carried out (see legend note).; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Fought at the heart of it to the end — sword snapped, thumb split open, saved by his chain mail.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Commanded the second column; arrived to cordon the streets, and pointedly kept the late-coming Aizu troops outside — the credit would be the corps' alone.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Served in Hijikata's column securing the surrounding streets (accounts of individual placements vary).; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Served in Hijikata's cordon squad outside the inn.; Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — In Hijikata's column, which combed the riverside inns before converging on the Ikedaya.; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo] — Went in with Kondō's handful; a sword stroke opened his forehead after he pushed back his headband in the heat.; Takeda Kanryūsai [p:takeda] — One of the handful who went in with Kondō, and rewarded for it.; Yamazaki Susumu [p:yamazaki] — Credited, with other investigators, with the surveillance of Furutaka Shuntarō that exposed the plot and led to the raid.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — In Hijikata's column, and listed among the men rewarded for the night.; Matsubara Chūji [p:matsubara] — In Hijikata's column, which searched the riverside inns before converging on the Ikedaya.; Tani Sanjūrō [p:tani] — In Hijikata's column; his younger brother Mantarō fought inside with Kondō.
Under torture, the arrested merchant-loyalist Furutaka Shuntarō reveals a plot: loyalist bands will fire Kyoto in the summer wind, and in the chaos carry the emperor off to Chōshū. On the night of the Gion festival's eve, the corps splits into two search columns to comb the riverside inn districts. Kondō's small party finds the conspirators — over twenty of them — in council upstairs at the Ikedaya. Rather than wait, Kondō attacks with a handful of men, announcing "official business!" at the door. Two hours of close-quarters slaughter follow in the dark, on stairs and in corridors, until Hijikata's column arrives and seals the block. Around eight loyalists are killed and over twenty arrested; the corps loses three, with others dying of wounds. Overnight, the Shinsengumi becomes the most famous — and most hated — sword in Japan. Historians still debate whether the raid delayed the Meiji Restoration or, by enraging Chōshū, hastened it.
Legend & disputed: Okita coughing blood on the Ikedaya floor is the most famous scene in Shinsengumi fiction — and is probably not what happened. Nagakura's account says only that Okita fell ill during the fight; heatstroke on a stifling festival night is a common modern reading.

### The Kinmon Incident [ev:kinmon]
When: August 20, 1864 · Where: Hamaguri Gate, Kyoto Imperial Palace
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Commanded in the field beside Kondō during the pursuit operations.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Led the corps into the field under Aizu's command.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito]; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura]; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada]; Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — His Aizu troops held the palace gates against Chōshū's attack.
Enraged by the Ikedaya and by its expulsion from court the previous year, Chōshū marches armies on Kyoto to "appeal" at the palace gates by force. The fiercest fighting erupts at the Hamaguri Gate, where Aizu and Kuwana troops buckle until Satsuma reinforcements swing the day. The Shinsengumi deploys under Aizu command and joins the pursuit of the broken Chōshū columns. The battle's cost falls on the city: fires spread for two days, burning tens of thousands of homes — the great "Donburi-yake." Chōshū is declared an enemy of the court, and the shogunate's punitive expeditions against it will consume the regime's remaining strength.
The corps is first posted on the riverbank at Kujō, south of the city, then sent in pursuit as the Chōshū forces break. The chase ends on Tennōzan, the hill south-west of Kyoto where the loyalist Maki Izumi and his companions kill themselves rather than surrender. For the corps, still mostly swordsmen, it is a first taste of a battle fought with guns and cannon on open ground.

### The petition against Kondō [ev:petition1864]
When: Autumn 1864 · Where: Konkai Kōmyō-ji, Kurodani — Aizu's Kyoto headquarters
People: Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Led the protest — and later told the story himself in his memoir.; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Signed the complaint.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Signed the complaint — the same Saitō who would later be Kondō's most trusted spy.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Signed the complaint.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Accused by his own founding comrades of treating them as retainers; reconciled with them at Katamori's order.; Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — Mediated in person and kept the corps from splitting.
After the Ikedaya, Kondō is a celebrity, and some of his oldest comrades think it has gone to his head. He is treating the men who founded the corps with him as his retainers rather than his equals. Six members — Nagakura Shinpachi, Harada Sanosuke, Saitō Hajime, Shimada Kai and two others — take the extraordinary step of going over his head: they submit a written complaint, listing his faults, to the Aizu domain at its Kyoto headquarters, the temple of Konkai Kōmyō-ji in Kurodani. They stake their lives on it: if the charges are found false, they are ready to commit seppuku themselves.
Matsudaira Katamori himself intervenes. He summons the two sides, scolds and soothes, and reconciles them over sake. The corps holds together. But its code does not forget: within weeks one of the six, Kuzuyama Takehachirō, is ordered to commit seppuku on another charge — and few at the time doubted the connection.
Legend & disputed: Almost everything about the petition comes from Nagakura's own later accounts, which naturally present the six in a good light. The link between the protest and Kuzuyama's death is an inference, not a documented fact.

### Recruiting in Edo: Itō Kashitarō joins [ev:recruit1864]
When: Autumn 1864 · Where: Fukagawa, Edo
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Came east to recruit and to lobby the shogunate's leaders; won Itō over and gave him senior rank.; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo] — Went ahead to Edo and, through his old school, brought Kondō to Itō — the most consequential introduction of his life.; Itō Kashitarō [p:ito] — Agreed to join with his followers and took the name Kashitarō to mark the year.; Suzuki Mikisaburō [p:suzuki] — Came west with his elder brother.; Shinohara Tainoshin [p:shinohara] — Came west with Itō as one of his closest followers.
After the Ikedaya and the Kinmon battle the Shinsengumi is famous, overstretched and short of men. In the autumn Kondō travels east to recruit, and to press the shogunate's senior officials to bring the shogun back to Kyoto. Tōdō Heisuke, who has gone ahead, brings him to the Fukagawa dōjō of Itō Kashitarō, a Hokushin Ittō-ryū master, scholar and poet with his own circle of followers.
Itō agrees to join, and comes west with his younger brother Suzuki Mikisaburō, Shinohara Taishinosuke and others; with other recruits gathered in Edo, the corps grows to well over a hundred men. Kondō is delighted with his catch and gives Itō rank above the unit captains. The recruitment brings the corps numbers and polish. It also brings in, as an honoured senior officer, a man whose first loyalty is to the emperor rather than the shogun.

### Yamanami's seppuku [ev:yamanami]
When: March 20, 1865 · Where: Mibu barracks, Kyoto
People: Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami] — Returned without resistance and died by the code he had helped write.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Sent as the pursuer — perhaps deliberately, as the one man Yamanami would never fight — and served as his second.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — As enforcer of the code, bears history's blame for the death; whether he sought it or grieved it is unrecorded.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo]
The general secretary leaves a note and rides east — desertion, under the code he helped establish, and punishable by death. The pursuer sent after him is Okita Sōji, the young man he half-raised, and Yamanami returns from Ōtsu without resistance, though escape was surely within his power. On March 20 he dies by seppuku at the barracks, Okita serving as his second. Why he left, and why he came back, remain the corps' most haunting questions: disillusionment with its hardening character, protest at the planned move to Nishi Honganji, or something more private. The people of Mibu, whose children knew him as "Yamanami the Buddha," grieved openly.
Legend & disputed: The farewell with his lover Akesato through the barracks' lattice window first appears in Shimozawa Kan's 20th-century semi-fictional chronicles.

### Headquarters at Nishi Honganji [ev:honganji]
When: Spring 1865 · Where: Nishi Honganji temple, Kyoto
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo]; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Managed the move and the expanded corps' organization into the unit system.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Given the 1st unit — the corps' sharpest sword at its head.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Given the 3rd unit.; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Given the 2nd unit.; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Given the 10th unit.; Matsubara Chūji [p:matsubara] — Given the 4th unit and the instructorship in jūjutsu; he would be dead by the autumn.; Takeda Kanryūsai [p:takeda] — Given the 5th unit and the instructorship in military science — a post the coming Western drill would soon make obsolete.; Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — Given the 6th unit.; Tani Sanjūrō [p:tani] — Given the 7th unit and the instructorship in the spear.; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo] — Given the 8th unit.; Suzuki Mikisaburō [p:suzuki] — Given the 9th unit.; Itō Kashitarō [p:ito] — Staff officer (sanbō) above the unit captains, and instructor in letters.; Yamazaki Susumu [p:yamazaki] — Inspector; when Matsumoto Ryōjun came to reform the barracks' hygiene, Yamazaki is said to have learned field medicine from him.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — A sergeant (gochō) leading a squad within one of the units.; Yoshimura Kan'ichirō [p:yoshimura] — Joined in 1865 in the recruiting drive in the east, and became a sword instructor.; Ogata Shuntarō [p:ogata] — Assistant vice-commander and instructor in letters.; Hattori Takeo [p:hattori] — Inspector, close to Itō's circle.; Shinohara Tainoshin [p:shinohara] — Inspector and jūjutsu instructor, and Itō's chief lieutenant.
Swollen with post-Ikedaya recruits to some two hundred men, the corps outgrows Mibu and moves its barracks into the compound of Nishi Honganji — head temple of a Buddhist sect with millions of adherents, and one suspected of quiet sympathy for Chōshū. The choice is deliberate intimidation as much as logistics. The monks endure drilling, gunfire, and reportedly pigs being raised and slaughtered on sacred ground for the corps' table (a menu encouraged by the physician Matsumoto Ryōjun for the men's health). The temple would eventually help finance the corps' next, purpose-built headquarters just to be rid of them.
Around this time the corps takes its mature shape: ten numbered units, each under a captain, and appointed instructors in the sword, spear, jūjutsu, military science, letters, gunnery and horsemanship. The corps' code is applied to its officers as harshly as to anyone. Of the new captains, Matsubara is dead by the autumn, Tani the following spring — both in circumstances the records do not explain — and Takeda, having left the corps, is hunted down and killed in 1867.

### Death of Matsubara Chūji [ev:matsubaradeath]
When: Autumn 1865 · Where: Kyoto — the corps at Nishi Honganji
People: Matsubara Chūji [p:matsubara] — Died, in circumstances the records do not explain.
Matsubara Chūji, captain of the 4th unit and the corps' jūjutsu instructor — the big, shaven-headed man the corps called its "modern Benkei" — dies within months of receiving his unit. The corps' records note the death and almost nothing else; some accounts say he had fallen from favour shortly before. He is the first of the new unit captains to die, and the silence around how he died says a good deal about the corps in its Honganji years.
Legend & disputed: The famous story that he died in a double suicide with the widow of a man he had killed is Shimozawa Kan's 20th-century invention. Where he died is not recorded; the map point marks the corps' headquarters at the time.

### Kondō's missions to Hiroshima [ev:hiroshima]
When: Winter 1865 – early 1866 · Where: Hiroshima
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Joined the inspector's suite and tried, without success, to be allowed into Chōshū.; Itō Kashitarō [p:ito] — Went as one of Kondō's two learned officers — and met Chōshū's men across the table.; Ogata Shuntarō [p:ogata] — Went with Kondō on both journeys, a sign of how far Kondō trusted him.
Its first expedition against Chōshū having ended without a battle, the shogunate is preparing a second, and first sends officials to Hiroshima, on Chōshū's border, to question the domain's envoys. Kondō goes with them, attached to the suite of the inspector-general Nagai Naomune, and takes Itō Kashitarō and Ogata Shuntarō, his most learned officers. He hopes to cross into Chōshū itself to see the enemy at first hand. Chōshū's representatives refuse him entry, and the talks go nowhere. Early in 1866 he makes a second journey with the shogunate's next delegation, with no better result.
The missions show how far the corps' commander has come: a swordsman from Tama now treated as a political agent. They also put Itō in the same rooms as Chōshū's men. That summer the shogunate's second expedition against Chōshū ends in defeat, and the balance of power in the country turns.
Legend & disputed: Who went on which of the two journeys varies between accounts; Itō's and Ogata's presence is the best attested.

### Death of Tani Sanjūrō [ev:tanideath]
When: Spring 1866 · Where: Stone steps of the Yasaka shrine, Gion
People: Tani Sanjūrō [p:tani] — Died suddenly here; the cause is not recorded.
Tani Sanjūrō, captain of the 7th unit and the corps' instructor in the spear, dies suddenly near the stone steps at the foot of the Yasaka shrine in Gion, one of the busiest corners of the city. The corps' records do not say how. His family's place close to Kondō — his youngest brother had been adopted as Kondō's heir — does not survive him, and the adoption is later dissolved.
Legend & disputed: Popular accounts say he was assassinated by his own comrades and often name Saitō Hajime; no contemporary record supports it, and illness is just as possible.

### The Sanjō notice-board incident [ev:sanjo]
When: October 1866 · Where: Sanjō Ōhashi bridge, Kyoto
People: Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Led one of the squads that sprang the ambush, and was in the thick of the fighting.
At the west end of the Sanjō bridge, where the Tōkaidō enters Kyoto, the shogunate posts a notice board naming Chōshū an enemy of the court. Someone keeps tearing it down and throwing it in the river. The corps is ordered to guard it. Over several nights some thirty men lie in wait in three squads, two of their own disguised as beggars by the bridge to watch.
After midnight a party of about eight samurai of the Tosa domain arrives and pulls the board down. The squads close in. In the fighting in the dark streets several of the Tosa men are cut down or taken and the rest escape. Harada Sanosuke's squad is in the thick of it, and the corps is rewarded by Aizu. The incident is small, but it shows which way the wind is blowing: a domain that had stood aside, Tosa, now has young men ready to fight the shogunate's police in the open.
Legend & disputed: The number of attackers, how many were killed or captured, and the exact placing of the squads differ between accounts.

### The split: Itō founds the Goryō Eji [ev:goryo]
When: April 1867 · Where: Gesshin-in, Kōdai-ji, Higashiyama, Kyoto
People: Itō Kashitarō [p:ito] — Engineered an honourable exit from a corps that allowed none, and led his followers out.; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo] — Left with Itō, the man he had recruited.; Suzuki Mikisaburō [p:suzuki] — Left with his brother.; Shinohara Tainoshin [p:shinohara] — Left with Itō as his chief lieutenant.; Hattori Takeo [p:hattori] — Left with Itō's party.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Left with Itō — as the corps' spy, it later emerged.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Let Itō go rather than defy an imperial appointment — and kept a man inside his camp.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Agreed the terms of the split; the corps' code was not set aside, only postponed.
Emperor Kōmei, the shogunate's most important friend at court, has died suddenly at the start of the year. Itō Kashitarō, who has never shared Kondō's loyalty to the shogun, sees his moment. Leaving the corps is desertion, and desertion means death, so Itō finds a door the code does not cover: with the backing of a court-connected temple he has himself and his followers appointed guards of the late emperor's tomb, the Goryō Eji. Kondō and Hijikata, unable to forbid an imperial appointment, let them go.
More than a dozen men leave, among them Tōdō Heisuke, Suzuki Mikisaburō, Shinohara Taishinosuke, Hattori Takeo — and Saitō Hajime. That summer they settle in Gesshin-in, a sub-temple of Kōdai-ji in the hills of Higashiyama, and are known in the city as the Kōdai-ji party. The two sides agree that from now on no one will move between them. Before the year is out, Itō and three of his men will be dead.
Legend & disputed: Whether Saitō went over as a planted spy from the start, or changed sides later, cannot be settled from the record; the corps' own side always presented him as its agent.

### Direct retainers of the shogun [ev:hatamoto]
When: June 1867 · Where: Fudōdō village, Kyoto
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Attains the dream of his life — samurai rank — months before it becomes a death sentence.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — The medicine peddler of Ishida village is now a retainer of the shogun.; Okita Sōji [p:okita]; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura]; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada]; Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — Made a direct retainer of the shogun with the other senior officers.; Ogata Shuntarō [p:ogata] — Made a shogunal retainer with the other senior officers.
The corps is elevated en masse into the shogun's direct service — Kondō with the standing of a yoriai-class retainer, Hijikata as a fukugoshin — and moves into a lavish new compound at Fudōdō, complete with a bathhouse said to fit thirty men, paid for in large part by Nishi Honganji's relief at their departure. For the Tama farmers it is the summit of a lifetime's climb: they are, at last and officially, samurai. The irony is total. The Tokugawa order that just ennobled them has months to live, and the rank will soon mark them not as gentlemen but as rebels.
Not everyone wanted the honour. A small group of members, who had hoped to follow Itō Kashitarō out of the corps rather than become the shogun's men, went to the Aizu residence to plead to be released. Four of them died there — by their own hands, the corps reported; the circumstances have been questioned ever since.

### Takeda Kanryūsai killed [ev:takedadeath]
When: Summer 1867 · Where: Bank of the Kamo river, Kyoto
People: Takeda Kanryūsai [p:takeda] — Killed by men of the corps after leaving it.
Takeda Kanryūsai, once captain of the 5th unit and the corps' instructor in military science, has left the Shinsengumi; his classical learning made obsolete by Western drill, he is suspected of seeking new patrons among the corps' enemies. In the summer of 1867 he is cut down on the bank of the Kamo river, near the Zenitori bridge, by men of the corps sent after him. For an officer who left and was suspected of dealing with the other side, the corps' code allowed only one outcome.
Legend & disputed: The date (some accounts put it in 1866), the exact place and the killers are all uncertain; popular accounts name Saitō Hajime. The map point is approximate.

### The Aburanokōji Incident [ev:aburanokoji]
When: December 13, 1867 · Where: Aburanokōji street, Kyoto
People: Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Returned from inside the Goryō Eji with the intelligence that set the ambush in motion — the corps' quietest and most consequential act of espionage.; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Fought in the street battle against the Goryō Eji come to claim the body.; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Fought in the ambush; his spear is prominent in most accounts of the night.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — The operation — banquet, killing, corpse-bait, ambush — bears his planning signature.; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo] — Came with the Goryō Eji to recover Itō's body and was killed in the fight, aged twenty-three.; Suzuki Mikisaburō [p:suzuki] — Itō's younger brother; among the Goryō Eji who escaped the ambush, taking refuge in the Satsuma residence.; Itō Kashitarō [p:ito] — Lured to a drinking party at Kondō's mistress's house and cut down near Aburanokōji on the way home; his body was left in the street as bait.; Hattori Takeo [p:hattori] — Stood his ground with a sword in each hand as the ambush closed; killed, his body covered in wounds.; Shinohara Tainoshin [p:shinohara] — Fought his way out of the ambush and escaped to the Satsuma residence.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Invited Itō to drink at his mistress's house on the night he was killed.
In 1867 the corps' brilliant, ambitious military adviser Itō Kashitarō had split off with over a dozen members — Saitō among them — to form the Goryō Eji, guards of the imperial tomb, drifting toward the loyalist camp. Saitō returns to the corps (almost certainly having been its informant all along) with word that Itō's group means to assassinate Kondō. The corps moves first: Itō is invited to a banquet, plied with drink, and cut down on his way home at Aburanokōji. His corpse is then left in the winter street as bait. When seven of his comrades come for the body, some forty Shinsengumi are waiting. Three of the Goryō Eji die in the ambush; the survivors' hatred will follow the corps to the end — one of them fires the shot that wounds Kondō weeks later, and another will identify him for the executioner at Itabashi.

### The Tenmaya incident [ev:tenmaya]
When: January 1, 1868 · Where: Tenmaya inn, Aburanokōji, Kyoto
People: Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Commanded the guard detail at the inn and fought off the attackers.
Sakamoto Ryōma has been assassinated in Kyoto, and his comrades in Tosa's Kaientai and Rikuentai are hunting for the men behind it. Their suspicion falls on Miura Kyūtarō, an official of the Kii domain, whose ship had collided with and sunk a Kaientai vessel earlier that year in a dispute Ryōma had won. The corps is asked to guard him, and a detail including Saitō Hajime is posted with him at the Tenmaya inn.
On the night of the attack the guards are drinking with Miura upstairs when some sixteen men burst in. In the fight in the cramped rooms Miura is wounded but survives; the corps has men killed and wounded, and the attackers lose men too before they withdraw. Saitō's appearance here, weeks after Aburanokōji, places him back inside the corps and at its sharp end — and the night is a reminder of how many enemies the Shinsengumi now has in Kyoto.
Legend & disputed: The numbers on each side and who led the corps' detail differ between accounts.

### Kondō shot at Sumizome [ev:sumizome]
When: January 12, 1868 · Where: Sumizome, Fushimi
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Shot through the right shoulder from ambush; stayed in the saddle, but never again fought with the sword.; Shinohara Tainoshin [p:shinohara] — Named among the Goryō Eji survivors who laid the ambush.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Too ill to fight, sent to Osaka with Kondō.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Took command of the corps at Fushimi in Kondō's place.
The shogun has returned his powers to the court and the shogunate's forces are gathering at Fushimi and Osaka. The corps has moved into the Fushimi magistrate's office. Riding back from a council in Kyoto, Kondō passes through Sumizome on the road south — and a musket fires from a house by the road. The ball smashes into his right shoulder. His escort is attacked and men are killed, but Kondō keeps his seat and his horse carries him back to Fushimi.
The gunmen are survivors of the Goryō Eji, taking revenge for Aburanokōji. Kondō is sent down to Osaka for treatment by the shogunate's physician Matsumoto Ryōjun, with the sick Okita Sōji. The wound never fully heals, and he will not hold a sword properly again. When the war begins two weeks later, it is Hijikata who leads the Shinsengumi into it.
Legend & disputed: Who fired the shot is disputed: the Goryō Eji survivors' own accounts name different men, and Shinohara's share is not certain.

### Battle of Toba–Fushimi [ev:tobafushimi]
When: January 27–31, 1868 · Where: Fushimi, south of Kyoto
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Commanded the corps in Kondō's absence, learning modern war the hardest way.; Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — Shot and killed at Yodo-Senryōmatsu on January 29, the first of the Tama comrades to die in battle. His young nephew tried to carry his head home.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Fought in the street fighting around the Fushimi magistrate's office.; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Fought at Fushimi; one of the corps' steadiest hands in the rout.; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Fought at Fushimi with the corps' forward elements.; Yamazaki Susumu [p:yamazaki] — Badly wounded in the retreat; died on the voyage to Edo or soon after.; Yoshimura Kan'ichirō [p:yoshimura] — Fought in the battle and vanished from the record afterwards.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Fought at Fushimi and in the retreat south.
The Boshin War opens. A shogunal army of some 15,000 marches on Kyoto to "clear the emperor's side of traitors" — meaning Satsuma — and collides with 5,000 Satsuma-Chōshū troops at Toba and Fushimi. Numbers prove worthless against drilled riflemen and artillery. The Shinsengumi fights in the burning streets around the Fushimi magistrate's office, where swords against Satsuma's guns achieve little but casualties. On the third day the imperial brocade banner is raised over the Satsuma lines: the shogunal army is now, formally, an army of traitors, and its commanders' will collapses. The shogun slips away to Edo by warship. The corps retreats with the army and sails east — Hijikata, by later accounts, concluding that the age of the sword ended in those streets.
At Fushimi the corps holds the magistrate's office until Satsuma guns on the higher ground above it set it on fire. In the retreat south, the Yodo domain — whose own lord is one of the shogun's senior councillors — shuts its castle gates against the beaten army, and at Yamazaki the Tsu domain's batteries, until then on the shogun's side, open fire on it from across the river. It is along this road, at Senryōmatsu by Yodo, that Inoue Genzaburō falls.

### Inoue Genzaburō falls at Senryōmatsu [ev:senryomatsu]
When: January 29, 1868 · Where: Senryōmatsu, on the Yodo river south of Fushimi
People: Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue] — Shot and killed in the fighting on the embankment, aged about forty.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Commanding the corps in the retreat.
On the third day of the Boshin War the shogunal army, driven out of Fushimi, tries to make a stand along the embankment of the Yodo river at Senryōmatsu, a stretch of pines on the road south. The Shinsengumi is in the line. Government riflemen push along the embankment and through the reed beds, and the line breaks.
Inoue Genzaburō, the oldest of the Shieikan men, is shot and killed. His nephew Inoue Taisuke, a boy of about twelve serving with the corps, tries to carry his uncle's head and sword back for the family, finds the weight too much in the rout, and buries them by the roadside near a temple. The place has never been found. Of the men who had marched out of Edo together, Inoue is the first to die in battle.
Legend & disputed: The story of Taisuke and his uncle's head comes down through the Inoue family; its outline is consistent, but the temple and exact spot vary. The modern placename Senryōmatsu-chō lies a little north of the old battle line; the map point is approximate.

### Back to Edo by sea [ev:shinagawa]
When: February 1868 · Where: Shinagawa, Edo
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Brought the corps home and began remaking himself — Western dress, short hair, and a new creed of firepower.; Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Shipped east with his wounded shoulder and placed in Matsumoto Ryōjun's care.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Brought back to Edo gravely ill.; Yamazaki Susumu [p:yamazaki] — Died of his wounds on the voyage — by the most common account, buried at sea.; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Returned with the survivors.; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Returned with the survivors.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Returned with the survivors.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Returned with the survivors.; Ogata Shuntarō [p:ogata] — Returned with the survivors.
After Toba–Fushimi the shogunal army falls back on Osaka castle — and then learns that the shogun has slipped away by night on a warship to Edo. The army breaks up. The Shinsengumi, its wounded and sick among them, is shipped east aboard the shogunate's vessels. Yamazaki Susumu, wounded in the retreat, dies on the voyage. The survivors land at Shinagawa, the post town at the southern gate of Edo, and are quartered at the Kamaya inn.
Of the men who had left Edo five years before, Inoue Genzaburō is dead, Kondō has a shattered shoulder and Okita is dying of tuberculosis; they are taken into the care of Matsumoto Ryōjun. Hijikata soon changes into Western clothes and cuts his hair short. By one account he tells a visitor that from now on war belongs to the gun, and that swords and spears are of no further use.
Legend & disputed: Which ships carried which men, the exact landing dates, and the place and manner of Yamazaki's death vary between accounts. Hijikata's remark about the gun comes from the recollection of the scholar Yoda Gakkai.

### Defeat at Kōshū-Katsunuma [ev:koshu]
When: March 29, 1868 · Where: Katsunuma, Kai province
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Commanded as "Ōkubo Tsuyoshi"; blamed by critics for the fatal delays on the march.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Left mid-campaign to seek reinforcements that never materialized.; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Fought the rearguard, then refused vassalage and parted with Kondō forever.; Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Fought at Katsunuma and left with Nagakura to form the Seiheitai.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Fought at Katsunuma, then went ahead to Aizu with the wounded.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Fought at Katsunuma.; Okita Sōji [p:okita] — By tradition, set out with the column but was too ill to go on and was left behind on the way.
Back in Edo, the corps is renamed the Kōyō Chinbutai — "Kai Pacification Force" — and sent to secure Kōfu castle ahead of the advancing imperial army. The march is a small tragicomedy: triumphal send-offs and feasting in the Tama homeland (Kondō traveling, critics said, like a daimyō) cost precious days, and the imperial vanguard reaches Kōfu first. At Katsunuma, some 120 men with a few guns face roughly ten times their number with modern artillery; the line holds for a couple of hours and dissolves. The defeat breaks more than a battle line. In its aftermath, Kondō asks the survivors to continue as his retainers — and Nagakura and Harada, sworn comrades but no man's vassals, walk away for good.

### The Goryō Eji dead laid to rest [ev:kaikoji]
When: 1868 · Where: Kaikō-ji, Sennyū-ji, Higashiyama
People: Itō Kashitarō [p:ito] — Reburied here with his comrades.; Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo] — Reburied here beside Itō.; Hattori Takeo [p:hattori] — Reburied here beside Itō.; Suzuki Mikisaburō [p:suzuki] — Among the survivors who reburied the dead.; Shinohara Tainoshin [p:shinohara] — Among the survivors who reburied the dead.
After the new government takes Kyoto, the surviving Goryō Eji recover the bodies of Itō Kashitarō, Tōdō Heisuke, Hattori Takeo and Mōnai Arinosuke, killed at Aburanokōji, and rebury them at Kaikō-ji, a sub-temple of Sennyū-ji — the temple of the imperial tombs that the Goryō Eji had been appointed to guard. The men who had left the Shinsengumi to serve the emperor lie, in the end, beside the emperors.
Legend & disputed: The exact date of the reburial varies between accounts.

### Kondō surrenders at Nagareyama [ev:nagareyama]
When: April 1868 · Where: Nagareyama, Shimōsa province
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Chose surrender over a hopeless fight — the last command decision of his life, made for his men.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Rode to Edo and spent everything — contacts, pride, pleading — trying to save his friend. It was not enough.; Sōma Kazue [p:soma] — One of the few who went with Kondō to the enemy camp when he gave himself up; detained for a time.
Rebuilding with fresh recruits at the river town of Nagareyama, the corps is surprised and surrounded by imperial forces. Battle means annihilation. Kondō — still under his alias — walks into the enemy camp to negotiate, presenting himself as a mere pacification officer, reportedly to buy his men's escape. It nearly works — until he is recognised at the enemy's headquarters at Itabashi. Hijikata races to Edo and moves every contact he has, including an appeal through the old shogunal leadership, to save him. He and Kondō had said goodbye at Nagareyama as if it were routine. Both, the accounts suggest, knew better. The two never saw each other again.

### Hijikata joins Ōtori's army at Kōnodai [ev:konodai]
When: May 1868 · Where: Kōnodai, Shimōsa province
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Joined Ōtori's army and was given command of its vanguard.
Edo castle has been surrendered to the new government, and thousands of shogunal soldiers who refuse to accept it slip out of the city. They gather at Kōnodai, a hill across the Edo river where the shogunate had drilled troops, and organise themselves into an army under Ōtori Keisuke, a Western-trained officer who had commanded the shogunate's modern infantry.
Hijikata arrives days after parting from Kondō at Nagareyama, bringing a few Shinsengumi men with him. Ōtori's army has drilled riflemen and French-style officers but few experienced field commanders, and Hijikata is put in charge of its vanguard. It is the beginning of his second career: no longer the police officer of Kyoto, but a commander of modern troops in open war.

### Hijikata storms Utsunomiya castle [ev:utsunomiya]
When: May 1868 · Where: Utsunomiya Castle, Shimotsuke
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Led the vanguard that stormed the castle; wounded in the foot days later when it was retaken.
Attached now to Ōtori Keisuke's old-shogunate column, Hijikata leads a vanguard against Utsunomiya castle and takes it by storm — the medicine peddler commanding professional soldiers, and well. The victory shows his new creed in action: firepower, discipline, momentum. A grim anecdote survives: when a soldier broke and ran, Hijikata cut him down on the spot to hold the line, then reportedly wept for him that night and paid for his memorial — the old corps' code, exported to a modern battlefield. Days later, in the fighting when imperial forces retake the castle, Hijikata is shot in the foot and carried north to Aizu to recover.

### Execution of Kondō Isami [ev:itabashi]
When: May 17, 1868 · Where: Itabashi, north of Edo
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Identified despite his alias, and beheaded as a criminal rather than allowed seppuku.; Sōma Kazue [p:soma] — Went with Kondō into captivity and was held for a time.; Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Years later, helped raise the memorial to Kondō and the corps' dead at Itabashi.
After his surrender at Nagareyama, Kondō is taken to the headquarters of the new government's army at Itabashi, the first post town on the Nakasendō. He still calls himself Ōkubo Yamato. But among the officers is Kanō Michinosuke, a former member of the corps who had left with Itō's Goryō Eji, and Kanō knows his face. Kondō is identified, interrogated and held for weeks while the victors argue over his fate.
Satsuma and Tosa men, who blame the corps for the deaths of their comrades — Sakamoto Ryōma among them, wrongly as it later turned out — want him dead. On May 17 he is beheaded on the execution ground at Itabashi as a common criminal, denied the samurai's right to seppuku. He is thirty-three. His head is sent to Kyoto and displayed on the bank of the Kamo river. A memorial to him and the corps' dead now stands near the spot, raised in the Meiji era with the help of Nagakura Shinpachi.
Legend & disputed: Kondō's death poem, and accounts of his calm at the execution ground, come from later sources. Where his body lies is uncertain: besides Itabashi, a grave at Ryūgen-ji in Mitaka is said by family tradition to hold remains recovered by his relatives.

### The corps at Shirakawa [ev:shirakawa]
When: June – July 1868 · Where: Shirakawa, gateway to the north
People: Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Commanded the corps in the fighting, as Yamaguchi Jirō.
Shirakawa, with its castle on the Ōshū highway, is the gate between the Kantō plain and the north. As the new government's army advances on Aizu, the northern domains and Aizu make their stand here, and the Shinsengumi — reduced to a few dozen men, with Hijikata recovering from his wound — fights under Saitō Hajime, now going by the name Yamaguchi Jirō.
The defenders hold the town at first and throw back an early assault. Then a smaller, better-armed government force takes the castle in a single day's fighting, and repeated attempts to retake it fail with heavy losses. The north's front door is open. Over the summer the government armies push on, and the corps falls back towards Aizu with them at its heels.

### Death of Okita Sōji [ev:okitadeath]
When: July 19, 1868 · Where: Sendagaya, Edo
People: Okita Sōji [p:okita] — Died in hiding, unaware that the master he kept asking after was already two months dead.
While Hijikata fights in the north, the corps' brightest sword dies quietly in a gardener's cottage in Sendagaya, where he has been hidden under the care arranged by the physician Matsumoto Ryōjun. Those tending him have kept Kondō's execution secret; Okita is said to have asked after his master to the end. He is about 26. A famous story from his last days has him repeatedly trying, and failing, to cut down a black cat that crossed the garden — the swordsman measuring, in the only units he knew, how little of himself remained.
Legend & disputed: The black cat story comes from later reminiscence and may be embellished; it has become inseparable from his legend.

### The Aizu campaign [ev:aizu]
When: Autumn 1868 · Where: Aizu-Wakamatsu
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Fought the defensive campaign, then moved north to continue the war with Enomoto's forces.; Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Refused to leave, fought Aizu's last battles with a dozen men, and shared the domain's surrender and exile.; Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — Defends Tsuruga castle through a month-long siege and surrenders in November 1868.; Ogata Shuntarō [p:ogata] — Last clearly recorded with the corps here; what became of him afterwards is unknown.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Fought through the Aizu campaign, then went north with Hijikata.
Aizu — the domain that took in the masterless swordsmen of Mibu five years earlier — is the imperial coalition's designated villain, and its punishment is total war. Hijikata, recovered from his wound, fights in the doomed defensive campaign as the ring closes on Wakamatsu castle. The remnant Shinsengumi under Saitō fights at Bonari Pass and beyond. When the strategic decision comes to fall back and continue the war from the north, Saitō refuses: to abandon Aizu in its extremity, he reportedly says, would betray everything the corps had claimed to stand for. About a dozen men stay with him. He and Hijikata — the corps' two hardest men, choosing opposite duties — never meet again.

### Hijikata joins Enomoto's fleet [ev:sendai]
When: October 1868 · Where: Sendai
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Joined the fleet's leadership councils as a field commander of proven reputation.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Sailed north with Hijikata.; Mori Tsunekichi [p:mori] — Came north with the party of the Kuwana lord, Matsudaira Sadaaki, who sailed with the fleet.; Ōno Uchū [p:ono] — Followed his domain's heir, the former senior councillor Ogasawara Nagamichi, who sailed with the fleet.
At Sendai, as the northern alliance collapses domain by domain, Hijikata boards the runaway shogunal fleet of Admiral Enomoto Takeaki — eight warships that refused to be handed over to the new government, carrying some two thousand soldiers, French military advisers among them. In the councils aboard, the one-time farm boy is treated as a proven field commander; there is an account of officers proposing him for overall command of land operations. The fleet weighs anchor for Ezo (Hokkaido), carrying the last armed remnant of the Tokugawa order — the Shinsengumi now a company-sized unit among them — toward one final improvisation: a republic at the edge of the map.

### Saitō's last stand at Nyoraidō [ev:nyoraido]
When: October 1868 · Where: Nyoraidō, outside Aizu-Wakamatsu
People: Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Held Nyoraidō until the band was overrun; reported dead, he survived to share Aizu's surrender and exile.
Hijikata has gone north; Saitō and the men who would not abandon Aizu have stayed. With the castle town under siege, they hold a position at the small temple of Nyoraidō outside Wakamatsu. In October a government force falls on them. The little band is overwhelmed and scattered, and word spreads that Saitō and his men have all been killed.
They have not. Saitō and several others survive and fight on until Aizu surrenders in November, then go with the domain's men into captivity. When the defeated domain is moved north to the bleak lands of Tonami, at the tip of Honshu, Saitō goes with it. He will spend the rest of his long life among Aizu people — his wife was from an Aizu family — and later serve the new government as a Tokyo policeman under the name Fujita Gorō.
Legend & disputed: Who fought at Nyoraidō, and how many died, is uncertain; the report of Saitō's death that circulated shows how confused the news was even then.

### Landing at Washinoki [ev:washinoki]
When: December 3, 1868 · Where: Washinoki, Ezo
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Led the column that took the coast road to Hakodate.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Landed with the corps.; Mori Tsunekichi [p:mori] — Landed with the corps' Kuwana men.; Ōno Uchū [p:ono] — Landed with the corps.
After a stormy crossing from the north of Honshu, Enomoto's fleet anchors in the bay at Washinoki, on the coast north of Hakodate, and puts some three thousand men ashore in snow. Rather than attack Hakodate from the sea, the army marches on it overland in two columns: Ōtori Keisuke's by the main road through the hills, and Hijikata's by the coast road to the east.
The Shinsengumi — now a mix of Kyoto veterans and men who joined in the north, including retainers of the Kuwana and Karatsu domains — lands with the army. Within a week, after brief fighting, both columns reach Hakodate, the new government's officials flee across the strait, and the star fortress of Goryōkaku is in the fleet's hands.

### The capture of Matsumae [ev:matsumae]
When: December 18, 1868 · Where: Matsumae castle, Ezo
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Commanded the column that took Matsumae castle and pressed on to Esashi.
Hakodate and Goryōkaku have fallen to the fleet's army after only brief fighting, but the Matsumae domain, the only daimyō house on Ezo, has declared for the new government. Hijikata leads a column of some seven hundred men west along the winter coast. Matsumae castle — rebuilt only fifteen years earlier to face foreign ships at sea — has its guns pointed the wrong way, and Hijikata's men take it from the landward side in a single day. The domain's lord flees across the strait.
The campaign goes on to Esashi, where disaster strikes not on land but at sea: the Kaiyō Maru, the fleet's great Dutch-built flagship, sent up the coast in support, is driven onto the rocks by a winter storm and lost. Hijikata is said to have watched her go down from the shore. Ezo is now in the fleet's hands, but the republic has lost its strongest ship before its first election.
Legend & disputed: The story that Hijikata, watching the Kaiyō Maru founder, struck a pine tree in grief belongs to tradition; the tree is still pointed out in Esashi.

### The Republic of Ezo [ev:goryokaku]
When: December 1868 – January 1869 · Where: Goryōkaku fortress, Hakodate
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Led the landing column and the winter conquest of Matsumae; elected army magistrate in Japan's first (officers-only) election.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — One of Hijikata's closest followers in the last campaign.; Mori Tsunekichi [p:mori] — Kuwana retainer who took command of the reformed corps as a chief officer (頭取).; Ōno Uchū [p:ono] — Chief officer of the corps, then on Hijikata's staff.; Sōma Kazue [p:soma] — Rejoined the corps at Hakodate after his release.
The fleet lands on Ezo in early winter. Hijikata commands one of the two columns that converge on Hakodate and the Western-style star fortress of Goryōkaku, and then the winter campaign that takes Matsumae and clears the island. In January 1869 the officers hold Japan's first election — votes among themselves, but an election — and constitute the "Republic of Ezo" under President Enomoto. Hijikata is chosen as a magistrate of the army, effectively a vice-minister of war. Foreign observers in the harbor take the new government seriously enough to deal with it de facto. The peddler of Ishida sanyaku has become a founding officer of a republic; it will last five months.

### Raid on Miyako Bay [ev:miyako]
When: May 6, 1869 · Where: Miyako Bay, Rikuchū
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Aboard the Kaiten as the senior land officer; held the operation together through its bloody failure and withdrawal.
The new government's fleet — anchored by the French-built ironclad Kōtetsu, against which Ezo has no answer — masses at Miyako Bay for the final push north. The republic gambles on audacity: three ships will slip in at dawn under false colors, lay alongside the ironclad, and take her by boarding — an abordage out of the age of sail, attempted against a modern warship. Storms strip the attack to a single ship, the Kaiten, with Hijikata aboard. She comes alongside; the boarding party leaps down onto the ironclad's deck — where a Gatling gun meets them. The raid fails in minutes with heavy losses, the Kaiten fighting her way out. Even enemy officers, the accounts say, spoke of the attempt with a kind of awe; Hijikata's composure through the slaughter enters legend.

### Hijikata holds the pass at Futamata [ev:futamata]
When: May 24 – June 4, 1869 · Where: Futamata pass, on the road from Esashi to Hakodate
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Held the pass against two major assaults and withdrew undefeated when the rest of the front collapsed.
The new government's army lands on Ezo in the spring of 1869 and advances on Hakodate by several roads. On the mountain road from Esashi, at the pass of Futamata, Hijikata waits with a few hundred men behind lines of earthworks and trenches dug into the hillside. The first assault comes on May 24 and is beaten off after fighting through the night. Ten days later the enemy returns in greater strength, and again the line holds.
It is the finest defensive action of Hijikata's career: his men, firing in relays from cover, hold a far larger force for days on end, and the earthworks can still be traced in the woods today. But the other roads are not held. When the coastal route falls and his position is in danger of being cut off, Hijikata is ordered back to Goryōkaku. He has not lost the pass. He leaves it because the war is being lost everywhere else.
Legend & disputed: Famous details — tens of thousands of rounds fired in a single night, rifles too hot to hold being cooled with buckets of water, Hijikata passing among the men with a cask of sake but allowing each only one cup — come from participants' later accounts and may have grown with retelling.

### Hijikata killed at Hakodate [ev:death]
When: June 20, 1869 · Where: Ippongi gate, Hakodate
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Killed riding to the relief of the corps' last cut-off men — in death as in life, the enforcer who would not abandon his own.; Ōno Uchū [p:ono] — Fighting near Ippongi with Hijikata on his last day; his account is a main source for how Hijikata fell.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Holding Benten Daiba with the corps — the position Hijikata rode out to relieve.; Sōma Kazue [p:soma] — Cut off with the corps; within days of Hijikata's death he was its commander.
The imperial general assault on Hakodate. The town falls street by street; a detachment is cut off at the Benten battery near the harbor — among them the last men of the Shinsengumi. Hijikata gathers a relief force and rides out through the Ippongi gate toward the fighting, by most accounts driving his men forward from horseback in the open, and is shot through the lower abdomen. He dies within hours, age 34, days before the republic's surrender. He had sent his photograph, a strand of hair, and his sword back to his family in Hino — a man settling his affairs. His burial place was deliberately concealed and has never been found. The death poem attributed to him: though his body rot on the isle of Ezo, his spirit would guard his lord in the east.
Legend & disputed: Multiple accounts of his final ride differ in detail (the exact spot, his last words, who recovered his body). The concealed grave has produced a century and a half of theories, none proven.

### The last of the Shinsengumi: Benten Daiba [ev:benten]
When: June 24, 1869 · Where: Benten Daiba fort, Hakodate harbour
People: Sōma Kazue [p:soma] — Took command of the corps in its last days and surrendered it with the battery.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Held the battery with the corps to the end and went into captivity.
Benten Daiba is a stone gun battery built out into Hakodate harbour to defend it against foreign warships. When the new government's general assault takes the town, the battery's garrison — including the last men of the Shinsengumi — is cut off. Hijikata dies riding to its relief. For four more days the fort holds out under bombardment from land and sea, its food and water running out.
Its commander is Nagai Naomune, the shogunate's former inspector-general — the official Kondō had accompanied to Hiroshima four years earlier. On June 24 Nagai surrenders the battery, and with it the Shinsengumi, now led by Sōma Kazue, who has taken command only days before. The corps that had marched out of Edo six years earlier as a band of hired swordsmen ends here, as prisoners of war. Goryōkaku itself surrenders three days later.
Legend & disputed: Sōma's exact title and the date he took command vary between sources; the letter of the physician Takamatsu Ryōun is often cited for his appointment.

### A photograph comes home to Hino [ev:ichimura]
When: Summer 1869 · Where: Satō Hikogorō's house, Hino
People: Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Sent his photograph and a lock of his hair home before the end, by a boy he refused to let die with him.
Weeks before his death, Hijikata ordered his young page Ichimura Tetsunosuke, a boy of about sixteen, to leave Hakodate on a foreign ship and carry a package to his family in Hino. Ichimura refused, wanting to die with the corps, and gave way only when Hijikata threatened to cut him down. He slipped through the lines, crossed to the mainland, and made his way south to the house of Satō Hikogorō, Hijikata's brother-in-law, where the Tennen Rishin-ryū had been taught all those years before.
He brought a photograph of Hijikata in Western uniform, taken in Hakodate, a lock of his hair, and a few lines of writing. The photograph is the image of Hijikata the world knows today. The Satō family hid Ichimura for a time and kept what he carried, and the family's descendants in Hino, who run a small museum in the house, still look after Hijikata's memory.
Legend & disputed: The scene in which Hijikata threatened to cut Ichimura down if he refused comes from the Satō family's account of what the boy told them. The date of his arrival in Hino is not certain.

### Mori Tsunekichi gives his life for Kuwana [ev:morideath]
When: Winter 1869 · Where: Remembered in Kuwana
People: Mori Tsunekichi [p:mori] — Took responsibility for Kuwana's resistance and was put to death in Tokyo.
After the surrender at Hakodate, the new government demands that each rebel domain name the men responsible for its resistance. For Kuwana, Mori Tsunekichi — the senior retainer who had followed his lord north and served as a chief officer of the Shinsengumi in Ezo — accepts that role. In the winter of 1869 he is put to death in Tokyo, by most accounts ordered to commit seppuku, so that his domain can be pardoned. He is remembered in Kuwana, the castle town on Ise Bay, for that sacrifice.
Legend & disputed: He died in Tokyo; the map point marks Kuwana, where he is remembered. The manner and date of his death, and the claim that he was the only man put to death for the Hakodate war, need checking against the full record.

### Exile to Tonami [ev:tonami]
When: 1870 · Where: Tonami domain — Gonohe, northern Honshu
People: Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Went north with the people of Aizu and lived for a time at Gonohe.; Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — His house survived as the domain of Tonami, under his infant son.
After its surrender Aizu was abolished. In 1870 the new government allowed the house of Matsudaira to survive as the tiny domain of Tonami, at the cold northern tip of Honshu, and some seventeen thousand people of Aizu moved there. The land was poor, the winters brutal, and many families went hungry; it is remembered in Aizu as a second punishment after the war.
Saitō Hajime, who had refused to abandon Aizu in 1868, went north with its people and lived for a time at Gonohe. Four years later, when the domains had been abolished altogether, he left for Tokyo and a new career in the police.

### Sōma Kazue in exile on Niijima [ev:niijima]
When: 1870–1872 · Where: Niijima, Izu islands
People: Sōma Kazue [p:soma] — Exiled here for about two years; by tradition he taught the island's children.
Sōma Kazue, the man who surrendered the Shinsengumi at Benten Daiba, is sentenced to exile on Niijima, one of the Izu islands south of Tokyo Bay. He spends about two years there. By local tradition he teaches reading and writing to the island's children and marries a local woman. Pardoned in 1872, he returns to the mainland with her and serves briefly as a government official.
In 1875 he takes his own life, aged thirty-two. Why, he never explained. He had been the last commander of the corps for a few days; it may be that he carried that for the rest of his short life.
Legend & disputed: His teaching and marriage on Niijima come from island tradition; the reasons for his suicide are unknown.

### Katamori at Nikkō [ev:katamorinikko]
When: 1880 · Where: Nikkō Tōshō-gū
People: Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — Chief priest of the Tōshō-gū in his last years.
Released from confinement, Matsudaira Katamori, the former Protector of Kyoto who had taken the masterless swordsmen of Mibu under his wing, is appointed chief priest of the Tōshō-gū at Nikkō — the great shrine to Tokugawa Ieyasu, founder of the shogunate. The man branded an enemy of the court in 1868 spends his last years tending the memory of the dynasty he had served.
He died in Tokyo in 1893. Among his belongings was found a letter from Emperor Kōmei, written thirty years before, thanking him for his loyalty. He had kept it all his life, and had never shown it to anyone.

### Nagakura teaches at Kabato prison [ev:kabato]
When: 1882–1886 · Where: Kabato prison, Tsukigata, Hokkaido
People: Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Sword instructor to the prison's guards for about four years.
Kabato was one of the great prisons built in the 1880s to hold the Meiji government's convicts — among them many of its political enemies — and to put them to work opening up the interior of Hokkaido. Its guards had to be trained, and for about four years their sword instructor was Sugimura Yoshie, the former Nagakura Shinpachi, captain of the Shinsengumi's second unit.
The building that housed the prison's offices is now a museum. For Nagakura it was a steady post far from Kyoto, in the government service of the regime he had once fought, teaching the sword to men who would never need it the way he had.

### A monument to the two heroes [ev:takahata]
When: 1888 · Where: Takahata Fudō temple, Hino
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — One of the two men commemorated.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — One of the two men commemorated, near his home village.; Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — Wrote the monument's title in his own hand.
Twenty years after Kondō's execution and Hijikata's death, their families and friends in Tama — led by Satō Hikogorō of Hino and Kojima Shikanosuke of Ono-ji village, who had backed the corps from the start — raise a great stone monument at Takahata Fudō, the great temple near Hijikata's home village. Its title, "Monument to the Two Heroes Who Died for Their Loyalty," was written by Matsudaira Katamori.
It was a bold thing to do. To the Meiji government the two men were rebels, and by local tradition permission was hard to win. That the people of Hino did it anyway, in stone, shows how they remembered the farmers' sons who had gone to Kyoto: not as murderers of loyalists, but as their own.

### Shimada Kai, the last guard [ev:shimada1900]
When: 1900 · Where: Nishi Honganji, Kyoto
People: Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — Served as a guard at the temple in his last years.
After his release from captivity, Shimada Kai, the giant sergeant who had followed Hijikata to Hakodate, came back to Kyoto. He tried his hand at small businesses without much success, and then found work as a night guard at Nishi Honganji — the temple whose courtyards the corps had filled with drilling men and gunfire thirty years before.
He kept the corps' memory there for the rest of his life, and kept the diary in which he had recorded its movements from Kyoto to Hakodate. He died in 1900, aged seventy-two, still in the temple's service.
Legend & disputed: Accounts differ on whether he died at the temple while on duty, as a popular story has it, or at home.

### Nagakura tells the story [ev:otaru]
When: 1913 · Where: Otaru, Hokkaido
People: Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura] — Told the corps' story to the Otaru Shimbun in 1913 — the source behind most of its famous scenes.; Shimada Kai [p:shimada] — His diary of the corps' movements from Kyoto to Hakodate is the other great survivor's record.
Forty-four years after Hakodate, the Shinsengumi is remembered in Japan, if at all, as a band of murderers who fought the Restoration. In Otaru, a port town in Hokkaido, a reporter for the Otaru Shimbun sits down with an old swordsman living in the town: Sugimura Yoshie, born Nagakura Shinpachi, captain of the 2nd unit. Through the spring of 1913 the paper runs his recollections in instalments, from the Shieikan to the end.
Nagakura is one of a handful of survivors who left accounts: Shimada Kai kept a diary; Nishimura Kanefumi, who had watched the corps from Nishi Honganji, wrote a history from the Kyoto side; Nagakura had already written a memoir of his own. His newspaper story, though, is the voice in which most of the corps' famous scenes first reached the public, and almost every later history and novel draws on it. He dies in 1915, aged seventy-five.
Legend & disputed: Nagakura was telling the story half a century later, as the corps' chief survivor and with his own reputation to protect. Historians treat his account as indispensable and as needing to be checked wherever possible.

### Saitō Hajime laid to rest in Aizu [ev:fujitagoro]
When: September 28, 1915 · Where: Amida-ji, Aizu-Wakamatsu
People: Saitō Hajime [p:saito] — Died in Tokyo as Fujita Gorō and was buried in Aizu.
Fujita Gorō — Saitō Hajime — dies in Tokyo at the age of seventy-one, nine months after Nagakura. He had served the Meiji state as a policeman and a school guard and lived quietly among the people of Aizu, saying almost nothing of the Shinsengumi.
He is buried at Amida-ji in Aizu-Wakamatsu, the temple where the domain's war dead of 1868 were finally allowed to be buried after months lying where they fell. The last captain of the corps lies among the people he had refused to abandon.

## National events (context)

### Perry's Black Ships arrive [ev:perry]
When: July 1853 · Where: Uraga, Edo Bay
Commodore Perry's squadron steams into Edo Bay and forces open a country closed for two centuries. The shogunate's helplessness before the ships shatters its authority and ignites the movement that defines the next fifteen years: sonnō-jōi, "revere the emperor, expel the barbarians." Every actor in the Shinsengumi's story — the corps itself, the shishi it hunted, the domains that destroyed the shogunate — is set in motion by this moment. Hijikata is eighteen, selling medicine in Tama.

### Assassination at the Sakurada Gate [ev:sakuradamon]
When: March 24, 1860 · Where: Sakurada Gate, Edo Castle
Rōnin from Mito — the ideological homeland of Serizawa Kamo — cut down the shogunate's strongman, Great Elder Ii Naosuke, in the snow outside Edo castle, in revenge for his purges and his treaties with the West. The lesson lands across Japan: a handful of committed swordsmen can decapitate the state. Political terror becomes a standard instrument, and Kyoto will soon fill with men holding that conviction — creating precisely the disorder the Shinsengumi will be hired to suppress.

### The Namamugi Incident [ev:namamugi]
When: September 14, 1862 · Where: Namamugi village, Tōkaidō
Satsuma samurai in the daimyō's procession cut down a British merchant who fails to dismount. The reprisal — the Royal Navy's bombardment of Kagoshima a year later — teaches Satsuma the actual balance of power, and the domain pivots from "expel the barbarians" to buying British ships and guns. This quiet conversion of the loyalist camp from xenophobia to modernization is the strategic story of the decade: the Shinsengumi will fight the 1860s with swords against enemies who drew this lesson early.

### Aizu takes up the guard of Kyoto [ev:shugoshoku]
When: February 12, 1863 · Where: Konkai Kōmyō-ji, Kurodani, Kyoto
People: Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — Accepted the post against his advisers' warnings and entered Kyoto.
To restore order in a capital terrorised by loyalist assassins, the shogunate creates a new post, Protector of Kyoto, and presses it on Matsudaira Katamori, the young lord of Aizu. His advisers warn him that it will ruin the domain; he accepts out of duty to the Tokugawa house. He enters Kyoto with a thousand men and makes his headquarters at the hilltop temple of Konkai Kōmyō-ji. Within weeks, the masterless swordsmen of Mibu will be placed under his protection — the start of a bond between Aizu and the Shinsengumi that will last to the end.
Legend & disputed: The retainers' warning that the post would be like carrying firewood into a fire is a well-known tradition of the Aizu side.

### The shogun comes to Kyoto [ev:shogunkyoto]
When: April 21, 1863 · Where: Nijō castle, Kyoto
People: Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — As Protector of Kyoto, responsible for the city's order during the shogun's stay.
The young shogun Tokugawa Iemochi enters Kyoto — the first shogun to come in person since 1634. He comes as a supplicant: the court, under the sway of the loyalist radicals, extracts from him a promise to expel the foreigners by a fixed date that the shogunate has no means of keeping. It is to guard this visit that the Rōshigumi was raised, and the Mibu men's first duties are escorting his processions.

### The Coup of 8/18 [ev:coup818]
When: September 30, 1863 · Where: Kyoto Imperial Palace
People: Kondō Isami [p:kondo] — Marched with the corps in its first deployment under arms.; Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata] — Marched with the corps; the day's service won it the name Shinsengumi.; Serizawa Kamo [p:serizawa] — Led the corps to the palace; an anecdote has him facing down Aizu troops who barred the way, iron fan in hand.; Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — As Protector of Kyoto, planned the coup with Satsuma and put his troops on the palace gates.; Niimi Nishiki [p:niimi] — Turned out with the corps, still one of its commanders.
Aizu and Satsuma, in a dawn palace coup, expel Chōshū's troops and its allied radical nobles from Kyoto, breaking loyalist control of the court overnight. The Mibu Rōshigumi turns out under arms to guard the palace approaches — its first real deployment. For its service that day, the corps receives from the court of the protector of Kyoto a new name: Shinsengumi, "the newly selected corps." The exiled Chōshū radicals scatter into hiding across the city; hunting them down becomes the corps' daily work, and leads directly to the Ikedaya.

### The Satsuma–Chōshū Alliance [ev:satcho]
When: March 1866 · Where: Kyoto (Satsuma residence)
In deepest secrecy, brokered by the Tosa rōnin Sakamoto Ryōma and Nakaoka Shintarō, the bitter rivals Satsuma and Chōshū — enemies since the coup of 8/18 and the Kinmon fighting — form a covert alliance. Satsuma will front British weapons purchases for blockaded Chōshū; each pledges the other support against the shogunate. Nothing about the corps' Kyoto patrols is different the next morning, yet everything is decided: the combination that will destroy the shogunate now exists. The Shinsengumi never manages to lay hands on Ryōma, though it hunts him.

### The shogun dies; the war on Chōshū fails [ev:iemochi]
When: August 29, 1866 · Where: Osaka castle
The shogunate's second expedition against Chōshū has gone badly wrong: on every front Chōshū's rifle units, drilled in Western tactics and quietly supplied through Satsuma, beat the shogun's larger armies. In the middle of the campaign Iemochi, aged twenty, dies at Osaka castle. The fighting is called off under cover of mourning. Hitotsubashi Yoshinobu succeeds him as the last shogun, and the whole country has seen that the Tokugawa can no longer win a war against a single domain.

### Death of Emperor Kōmei [ev:komei]
When: January 30, 1867 · Where: Kyoto Imperial Palace
People: Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori] — Lost the emperor who had trusted him above all others.
Emperor Kōmei dies suddenly at thirty-five, of smallpox by the official account. He had hated foreigners, but he had also trusted Aizu and wanted the shogunate preserved; with him the shogunate's friends lose their protector at court. His successor is a boy of fourteen, the future Emperor Meiji, and the court falls under the influence of men who want the Tokugawa gone. Within a year, the shogunate will be.
Legend & disputed: Rumours that Kōmei was poisoned circulated at the time and have never quite died; most historians accept smallpox as the cause.

### Return of power to the emperor [ev:taisei]
When: November 9, 1867 · Where: Nijō Castle, Kyoto
Shogun Tokugawa Yoshinobu, maneuvering to preempt an armed overthrow, formally returns governing authority to the emperor at Nijō castle — betting that no one else can actually govern, and that a new council of lords will have to hand power back to him in practice. The Satsuma-Chōshū men understand the maneuver perfectly and prepare to close the loophole by force. For the Shinsengumi, elevated to shogunal retainers mere months earlier, the ground itself has moved: the institution they finally belong to has, on paper, just voted itself out of existence.

### Assassination of Sakamoto Ryōma [ev:ryoma]
When: December 10, 1867 · Where: Ōmiya, Kawaramachi, Kyoto
People: Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Falsely implicated by a scabbard left at the scene; the accusation followed the corps for decades.
A month after the return of power he helped engineer, Sakamoto Ryōma is assassinated with Nakaoka Shintarō in his room above the Ōmiya soy merchant's shop. Suspicion falls immediately on the Shinsengumi — a scabbard at the scene is attributed to Harada Sanosuke, and Kondō is later interrogated about the killing before his execution. The evidence now points overwhelmingly to the Mimawarigumi, a rival shogunal police unit, but the corps pays reputationally then and ever after: history's most beloved shishi, laid at its door.

### Proclamation of imperial restoration [ev:osei]
When: January 3, 1868 · Where: Kyoto Imperial Palace
Satsuma-led forces seize the palace gates and a rump council proclaims the restoration of direct imperial rule — abolishing the shogunate outright and stripping Yoshinobu of office and lands. It is the coup that closes the loophole of the peaceful handover. Yoshinobu withdraws to Osaka with his army, tempers fray for three weeks, and provocations (including Satsuma-sponsored rōnin terror in Edo) do their work: the march that ends at Toba–Fushimi begins.

### Bloodless surrender of Edo [ev:edocastle]
When: May 3, 1868 · Where: Edo Castle
Negotiated between Katsu Kaishū for the shogunate and Saigō Takamori for the imperial army, Edo — the largest city on earth — changes hands without a battle. Yoshinobu retires into watched seclusion. The settlement saves a million townspeople and dooms the irreconcilables: with the legitimate leadership standing down, men like Hijikata who fight on are no longer soldiers of a government but rebels by definition. The war's remaining course — Ueno, Aizu, Hakodate — is the story of those who could not accept the surrender.

### Battle of Ueno [ev:ueno]
When: July 4, 1868 · Where: Kan'ei-ji, Ueno, Edo
People: Harada Sanosuke [p:harada] — Turned back from the northern march — perhaps to see his wife and son — joined the Shōgitai, and was mortally wounded; he died two days later, aged 28.
The Shōgitai — thousands of shogunal loyalists garrisoning the great temple hill of Ueno in defiance of the surrender — are destroyed in a single day. Ōmura Masujirō's plan uses Armstrong guns firing across the Shinobazu pond to methodically demolish the position: a demonstration, weeks after Edo's peaceful handover, of exactly what resistance now costs. Among the dead of the following days is Harada Sanosuke of the Shinsengumi, who had turned back from the march north and joined the doomed garrison.

### Surrender of Goryōkaku — the Boshin War ends [ev:surrender]
When: June 27, 1869 · Where: Goryōkaku, Hakodate
A week after Hijikata's death, Enomoto surrenders Goryōkaku and the Republic of Ezo dissolves. The Boshin War is over; the Meiji state's authority now runs unbroken from Kyushu to Ezo. The epilogue is stranger than the war: within a few years Enomoto and other republic leaders are pardoned and serving the Meiji government as ministers and admirals, while Saitō Hajime patrols Tokyo as a policeman. The order the Shinsengumi died for became, with remarkable speed, a memory its survivors administered.

## People

### Hijikata Toshizō [p:hijikata]
Role: Vice-commander (fukuchō) · Life: 1835–1869
The organiser of the Shinsengumi. A farmer's son from the Tama hills, he built and enforced the corps' iron discipline as its "demon vice-commander," kept Kondō at its head, and then remade himself as a modern field commander in the Boshin War, fighting on for a year after his friend's death to the last battle at Hakodate.
**Ishida village** Hijikata was born on May 31, 1835, the youngest of ten children of a prosperous farming family in Ishida village, Musashi province — today part of Hino, west of Tokyo. His father died of tuberculosis before he was born, and his mother when he was five; he was brought up by his elder brother's household. Family tradition says he was sent to Edo as a boy to apprentice in a large dry-goods shop and came home after a quarrel, the first of several attempts to make a merchant of him that did not take.
As a young man he travelled the Tama countryside selling the family's patent remedy for bruises and sprains, Ishida sanyaku, with practice gear tied to his pack. His sister Nobu had married Satō Hikogorō, the headman of the post town of Hino, who kept a practice hall for the Tennen Rishin-ryū in his own house. The school's masters came out from Edo to teach there, and there Hijikata came to know Kondō Isami, a year his senior, and the boy prodigy Okita Sōji. In 1859 he formally entered the school under its third master, Kondō Shūsuke.
**To Kyoto** When the shogunate called for swordsmen to guard the shogun in Kyoto in 1863, Hijikata went west with the rest of Kondō's household as an unranked recruit in the Rōshigumi. When the column turned back, he was among those who stayed, and within months he held the title of vice-commander beside Yamanami Keisuke, with Kondō as one of three commanders.
The arrangement did not last. The faction of Serizawa Kamo was disgracing the corps, and in the autumn of 1863 Serizawa's lieutenant Niimi Nishiki was forced to commit seppuku and Serizawa himself was killed in his bed at the Yagi house. Most accounts place Hijikata at the head of the killers. From then on the corps belonged to Kondō's household — and its daily running belonged to Hijikata.
**The demon vice-commander** In Kyoto Hijikata became the corps' architect. He organised it into numbered units under captains with appointed instructors, ran its intelligence work through the inspectors, and enforced its internal code — under which leaving the corps, private quarrels and raising money without authority could all mean seppuku. Members who broke it died, officers among them: the general secretary Yamanami in 1865, the captain Takeda Kanryūsai in 1867. Hijikata is remembered as the man who demanded those deaths. The nickname "demon vice-commander" fits the record, even if the word was later writers' as much as his men's.
On the night of the Ikedaya in July 1864, he commanded the second search column and arrived to seal off the streets, keeping the late-coming Aizu troops outside so that the credit would be the corps' alone. In 1867, when the corps was made direct retainers of the shogun, the medicine peddler of Ishida village became a shogunal retainer himself.
**Letters home** Hijikata's letters to his family and friends in Tama show a different man. In one from 1863 he lists, with obvious pleasure, the women of Shimabara, Gion and Osaka who had sent him love letters, and adds a mock-solemn haiku about women who make a patriot forget his duty. Before leaving for Kyoto he had gathered his own verse into a small collection under the pen name Hōgyoku. The poems are earnest and mostly unremarkable — the best known, "a plum blossom: even one bloom, it is still plum," has been teased by readers ever since.
What the letters show above all is how much he wanted the people of Hino to know that their boys were doing well. The severity in Kyoto was an instrument he chose. His loyalty to Kondō, the friend he kept at the head of the corps, never wavered.
**The Hino circle** The corps never forgot where it came from, and neither did Tama. Hijikata's brother-in-law Satō Hikogorō, the headman of Hino, and Kojima Shikanosuke, headman of the village of Ono-ji, sent money, news and recruits west, and received in return the long letters that now tell us most of what we know about the corps' first years. When the Kōyō Chinbutai marched through Tama in 1868, Satō raised a small company of local men to go with it, and after the defeat at Katsunuma he had to go into hiding.
These men were Hijikata's real public. Everything he did in Kyoto, he did partly so that Hino would hear of it.
**The new war** The war that began at Toba–Fushimi in January 1868 changed him. With Kondō disabled by a gunshot wound, Hijikata led the corps through the fighting at Fushimi and the rout down the Yodo river, where Inoue Genzaburō was killed. Back in Edo he cut his hair, changed into Western clothes, and told a visitor that from now on war belonged to the gun.
The next months were defeats. On the march to Kōshū he went to seek reinforcements that never came; at Nagareyama, when Kondō chose to give himself up, the two said goodbye as if it were routine. Hijikata rode to Edo and tried every contact he had to save his friend, without success. He then joined the old shogunate's forces heading north, led the vanguard that stormed Utsunomiya castle, and was shot in the foot when the government army retook it. Recovering in Aizu, he is said to have raised a grave for Kondō at the temple of Tennei-ji.
**The north and Ezo** When Aizu's defence collapsed in the autumn of 1868, Hijikata went north to Sendai and joined the fleet of Enomoto Takeaki, while Saitō Hajime and others stayed to fight for Aizu. In Ezo he commanded the column that took Hakodate and Goryōkaku, then led the winter campaign that captured Matsumae castle and cleared the island. When the officers voted for the leaders of their new Republic of Ezo in January 1869, he was chosen as a magistrate of the army, its second-highest land commander.
That spring, when the government army landed, he held the mountain pass at Futamata against two major assaults and gave it up only when the other roads had fallen. In the weeks before the end, he sent his young page Ichimura Tetsunosuke through the lines with his photograph and a lock of his hair for his family in Hino.
**Death at Hakodate** On June 20, 1869, the government army made its general assault on Hakodate. The town fell quickly, and the battery at Benten Daiba, held in part by the last men of the Shinsengumi, was cut off. Hijikata gathered a small force and rode out through the Ippongi gate to relieve it. By most accounts he was urging his men forward from horseback when he was shot through the abdomen. He died within hours, aged thirty-four. The republic surrendered a week later.
His body was buried secretly by his comrades, and the place has never been found. A death poem is attributed to him: though his body might rot on the island of Ezo, his spirit would guard his lord in the east.
**Character** Contemporaries describe a man of two registers. In public he was cold, exacting and merciless in enforcing the code; members feared him, and his nickname was earned. In private he was orderly, literate and quietly humorous, a man who wrote long, teasing letters home and fussed over his poems. He was by all accounts good-looking, fastidious about his appearance, and proud — the boy who walked out of an apprenticeship after a quarrel never learned to bow easily.
War softened him, or showed another side. At Utsunomiya he cut down a soldier who ran, then is said to have paid for the man's memorial. In Ezo the soldiers under him, many with no tie to the old corps, came to trust him; one later account says they loved him like a mother. He never married; the corps was his household.
**Comrades** Kondō Isami was the fixed point of his life. Hijikata could have led the corps himself; instead he made Kondō its commander and spent five years keeping him there, doing the work that made Kondō look like a general and absorbing the blame for the deaths that held the corps together. When Kondō gave himself up at Nagareyama, Hijikata spent everything he had trying to save him.
Okita Sōji was something like a younger brother to both of them, and, by tradition, the one man in the corps who could tease Hijikata with impunity. Inoue Genzaburō, the steady elder from neighbouring Hino, had known him since boyhood. With Yamanami Keisuke, his fellow vice-commander and opposite in temperament, relations were correct but distant, and Yamanami's death in 1865 is the one most often laid at Hijikata's door. Itō Kashitarō he distrusted from the start. Saitō Hajime he trusted with the corps' most confidential work, and the two parted in Aizu in 1868, each convinced he was choosing the right duty.
In the north his comrades changed. He served beside Ōtori Keisuke, a Western-trained officer whose losses he sometimes had to repair, and under Enomoto Takeaki in Ezo. Shimada Kai, the giant sergeant from Kyoto, followed him to the end; the boy Ichimura Tetsunosuke, his page, carried his photograph home.
**His sword** Hijikata's best-known sword is a blade by Izumi-no-kami Kanesada, a smith of the Aizu domain's line, said to have been given to him in Kyoto. Family tradition holds that it was among the things sent home to Hino in his last weeks, and a Kanesada blade associated with him is kept today at the museum in his birthplace.
**Remembered** For decades after 1869 Hijikata was officially a rebel. In 1888 his family and friends in Tama nevertheless raised a great monument to him and Kondō at Takahata Fudō near his home, its title written by Matsudaira Katamori. In Hakodate, a memorial marks the area near the Ippongi gate where he fell, and visitors still leave flowers there.
The Hijikata family kept his belongings and opened a small museum at the site of his birthplace in Ishida. His fame beyond the circle of enthusiasts came later, above all through Shiba Ryōtarō's novel Moeyo Ken in the 1960s, which made the farmer's son who became a soldier one of the best-loved figures of Japanese history.
Legend & disputed: The famous five articles of the corps' code survive only in later accounts, and the claim that Hijikata wrote them is tradition. So is the scene in which he personally tortured Furutaka Shuntarō before the Ikedaya. The death poem is attributed to him by later writers, and the many theories about where his body lies — at Goryōkaku, in a Hakodate temple, or elsewhere — remain unproven.

### Kondō Isami [p:kondo]
Role: Commander (kyokuchō) · Life: 1834–1868
Commander of the Shinsengumi and fourth master of the Tennen Rishin-ryū. A farmer's son with a samurai's ambitions, he rose from a suburban dōjō to audiences with daimyō and court nobles and to the rank of a shogunal retainer — and was beheaded as a criminal when the order he served collapsed.
**A farmer's son** Kondō was born Miyagawa Katsugorō on October 9, 1834, the third son of a well-off farming family in Kami-ishihara village, Musashi province — today part of Chōfu, west of Tokyo. His father was a patron of the Tennen Rishin-ryū and kept a practice hall at home, and the boy took to the sword early. A story told in the family has the teenage Katsugorō and his brother, woken by burglars, waiting until the thieves were leaving with their loads before falling on them — a show of judgment that impressed the school's third master, Kondō Shūsuke.
In 1849 Shūsuke adopted him as his heir. He took the name Kondō Isami, married Matsui Tsune, the daughter of a samurai family in Edo, in 1860, and in 1861 was installed as fourth master with a mock battle among the school's students at the Rokusho shrine in Fuchū. His daughter Tama was born the following year.
**The Shieikan** The Shieikan, the school's small dōjō at Ichigaya in Edo, was poor and its students were mostly farmers from Tama, where the masters taught on circuit. But Kondō drew around him an unusual household: the boy prodigy Okita Sōji as a live-in pupil, the scholarly Yamanami Keisuke, guests from other schools such as Nagakura Shinpachi and Harada Sanosuke, the steady Inoue Genzaburō, and Hijikata Toshizō from Ishida village. When the shogunate called for swordsmen in 1863, Kondō signed on with all of them.
**Household** Kondō left a wife and a small daughter in Edo when he went west. Tsune kept the house and the school going in his absence, and his letters to her and to the Tama patrons are full of instructions about family and money. By a story often told, he chose her for her plain looks and good sense rather than beauty, saying that a beautiful wife would be a distraction for a man with work to do.
Their daughter Tama later married Kondō Yūgorō, a son of the Miyagawa family who had been adopted as Kondō's heir, and who became fifth master of the Tennen Rishin-ryū. The school survived its most famous master.
**Commander** In Kyoto Kondō's group refused to return east with the Rōshigumi and was taken under the protection of Aizu. After the death of Serizawa Kamo in the autumn of 1863, Kondō was the corps' undisputed commander and its public face. The Ikedaya raid of July 1864, which he led in person, sword in hand, made him famous across the country. He wrote home proudly that his treasured blade, which he believed to be by the master smith Kotetsu, had come through the fight undamaged.
Success changed him, or so some of his oldest comrades thought. That autumn six of them, Nagakura Shinpachi among them, complained in writing to Aizu that Kondō was treating his fellow founders as retainers, and Matsudaira Katamori himself had to reconcile them. Kondō went east to recruit and brought back the learned Itō Kashitarō, whom he gave senior rank; he cultivated Aizu officials and court nobles, and in 1865 and 1866 travelled to Hiroshima with the shogunate's envoys to question Chōshū. In 1867 he was made a direct retainer of the shogun — the dream of his life, months before the shogunate fell.
**A farmer in politics** Kondō was more than a sword. From 1864 he wrote memorials to the shogunate's officials on national policy, arguing for a hard line against Chōshū and for the shogun's presence in Kyoto, and he moved in the company of Aizu's senior retainers and of court nobles. His letters home describe these meetings with evident pride. Some of the corps' own men, and many outsiders, found it absurd that the head of a police unit raised from rōnin should lecture the great on policy; Kondō saw it as the natural consequence of service. His calligraphy, in the confident hand of a man who had taught himself to write like a samurai, survives in several letters and scrolls.
**The fall** The last months in Kyoto were violent. In December 1867 the corps killed Itō and ambushed his followers at Aburanokōji; days later, survivors of Itō's group shot Kondō from ambush at Sumizome, smashing his right shoulder. He could not fight at Toba–Fushimi and sailed back to Edo with the wounded.
In Edo the corps was renamed the Kōyō Chinbutai and sent to hold Kōfu castle, with Kondō commanding under the name Ōkubo Tsuyoshi. The march through the Tama homeland turned into a celebration, the government army reached Kōfu first, and at Katsunuma the little force was broken in a few hours. Afterwards, when Kondō asked the survivors to serve him as his retainers, Nagakura and Harada refused and left.
**Nagareyama and Itabashi** Rebuilding with new recruits at Nagareyama in April 1868, the corps was surrounded. Kondō, now calling himself Ōkubo Yamato, went to the enemy camp to give himself up, reportedly to give his men the chance to get away. At the government army's headquarters at Itabashi he was recognised by Kanō Michinosuke, a former member who had left with Itō's Goryō Eji.
On May 17, 1868, after weeks of argument among his captors, he was beheaded at Itabashi as a common criminal, denied the samurai's right to seppuku. He was thirty-three. His head was taken to Kyoto and displayed on the bank of the Kamo river. Graves and memorials to him stand at Itabashi, at Ryūgen-ji in Mitaka near his birthplace, and at Tennei-ji in Aizu.
**Character** Generous, gregarious and a natural speaker, Kondō inspired a loyalty in his closest followers that outlasted him: Hijikata fought on for a year after his death, and Nagakura, who had once protested against his arrogance, helped raise the memorial at Itabashi eight years later. Critics inside and outside the corps found him vain and increasingly grand. Both things were true of a farmer's son improvising a samurai's life in an order that was falling apart. A famous party trick has him putting his whole fist into his mouth, in imitation, he said, of the great general Katō Kiyomasa.
**Comrades and rivals** Hijikata Toshizō was Kondō's other half. Kondō was the face, the speaker and the host; Hijikata ran the corps behind him. Their partnership lasted from the practice hall in Hino to the parting at Nagareyama, and nothing in the record suggests that either ever doubted the other.
Okita Sōji had grown up in Kondō's house, and Kondō treated him as a younger brother; Inoue Genzaburō, older than both of them, was the loyal senior of the school. In Tama, Kondō, Hijikata's brother-in-law Satō Hikogorō and Kojima Shikanosuke of Ono-ji were, by tradition, bound by a sworn brotherhood, and Kondō's letters to the two headmen are among the best sources for the corps.
Others were harder. Serizawa Kamo was a rival he deferred to and then let be destroyed. Nagakura Shinpachi and Harada Sanosuke, comrades from the Shieikan, protested against his arrogance in 1864 and walked away from him in 1868 rather than become his retainers; Nagakura nonetheless raised his memorial at Itabashi. Itō Kashitarō, whom he recruited and promoted, became his rival and then his victim. Above them all stood Matsudaira Katamori, the lord of Aizu, whom Kondō served with a devotion that Katamori returned, writing the title on the 1888 monument to him and Hijikata. The shogunate's physician Matsumoto Ryōjun, who cared for the corps' sick and treated Kondō's wound, was a friend.
**Remembered** Kondō has several graves. One stands near the execution ground at Itabashi, raised in 1876 with the help of Nagakura Shinpachi and the physician Matsumoto Ryōjun. Another, at Ryūgen-ji in Mitaka near his birthplace, is said by family tradition to hold his body, recovered secretly by his relatives. A third, at Tennei-ji in Aizu, is the grave Hijikata is said to have raised. In 1888 the people of Tama put up the monument at Takahata Fudō to him and Hijikata together. A marker now stands at his birthplace in Chōfu.
Legend & disputed: His treasured "Kotetsu" was almost certainly not by Kotetsu; forgeries of the famous smith were everywhere, and there is no sign Kondō ever knew. The burglar story and the fist-in-mouth trick come from family and later anecdote. Several places claim his head, which disappeared from Kyoto after it was displayed; none of the claims can be proven.

### Okita Sōji [p:okita]
Role: Captain, 1st unit · Life: 1842?–1868
The prodigy of the Shieikan and captain of the first unit — by most accounts the finest sword in the Shinsengumi. Cheerful and boyish off duty, fierce on it, he was cut down not by an enemy but by tuberculosis, dying in hiding in Edo at about twenty-five.
**A boy at the Shieikan** Okita was born in the Edo residence of the Shirakawa domain, where his father served as a low-ranking retainer; his birth year is given as 1842 or 1844. His father died when he was small, and the family's position passed to the husband of his elder sister Mitsu, Okita Rintarō. Mitsu, who largely raised him, would remain the person he wrote to most.
At about nine he entered the Shieikan as a live-in pupil and grew up in Kondō's house, half disciple and half younger brother. His talent was out of the ordinary. He is said to have earned his full licence in his teens and to have acted as the dōjō's head instructor, travelling to teach the farmers of Tama in place of his masters while still a young man.
**To Kyoto** He went west with Kondō's household in the Rōshigumi in 1863 — while his brother-in-law Rintarō, who had also enrolled, returned east with Kiyokawa and served in Edo in the Shinchōgumi. At Mibu the Yagi family's children remembered the young swordsman who played tag and blind man's buff with them in the temple grounds. He is named in most accounts among the men who killed Serizawa Kamo that autumn.
At the Ikedaya in July 1864 he was one of the handful who went in with Kondō. He cut down several men in the first fighting and then collapsed and had to be carried out. In March 1865 he was the rider sent after Yamanami Keisuke, the man who had been like an elder brother to him, and he served as Yamanami's second at his seppuku.
**Captain of the first unit** When the corps was organised into units in 1865, Okita took the first — the unit that went in first. He was also one of its instructors in swordsmanship, and accounts agree that the sunny young man became short-tempered and severe the moment he took up a practice sword; members found his lessons rougher than Kondō's. Tradition credits him with a three-stage thrust, the sandan-zuki, in which the strikes landed almost as one.
**Illness** Tuberculosis, the disease that had killed so many in Edo, took hold of him in the corps' last Kyoto years. By the end of 1867 he could no longer serve. After Kondō was shot at Sumizome in January 1868 the two were sent down to Osaka together to be treated by the shogunate's physician Matsumoto Ryōjun, and Okita sailed back to Edo with the wounded after Toba–Fushimi.
In Edo he was placed in Ryōjun's care. By tradition he set out with the Kōyō Chinbutai in March but could go no further than the first stages of the road. He was then hidden in the house of a gardener in Sendagaya, on the outskirts of the city, as the new government's army occupied Edo.
**Death** Okita died there on July 19, 1868. Those caring for him had kept Kondō's execution, two months earlier, from him; he is said to have asked after his master to the end. He was about twenty-five. He is buried at Senshō-ji, a temple in Azabu associated with his family; for years the grave was closed to the public because of the numbers his legend drew.
**Family and letters** Several of Okita's letters survive, most of them to Satō Hikogorō in Hino and to his family: short, cheerful, apologetic about not writing more, full of greetings to people at home. They show a young man who stayed close to the Tama circle that had raised him.
His sister Mitsu and her husband Rintarō went north with the Shinchōgumi to the Shōnai domain when Edo fell, and settled there. Mitsu lived until 1907 and was one of the few people who could still describe her brother as he had been.
**The man and the legend** Those who knew him remembered a tall, wiry young man with a dark complexion and a plain face, fond of jokes and of children, who laughed easily and was serious only with a sword in his hand. The beautiful, doomed youth of novels, manga and film is a later creation. The core of the legend — a once-in-a-generation talent extinguished before he was thirty — is entirely historical.
**Comrades** Okita grew up in Kondō's house, and Kondō was something between an elder brother and a father to him; Okita's letters show a deep, unselfconscious attachment, and his last questions were about Kondō. Hijikata, eight years older, was the other fixed figure of his youth. Yamanami Keisuke, the gentlest of the Shieikan men, was by every account close to him — which is what made it so hard that Okita was sent after him in 1865, and served as his second.
He drank and brawled with Serizawa Kamo's party in Osaka in the corps' first summer, and by most accounts was among the men who killed Serizawa that autumn. Inoue Genzaburō and the Tama patron Satō Hikogorō watched over him as family. In his last illness it was the physician Matsumoto Ryōjun who arranged his care, and his sister Mitsu, far away in Shōnai, who was told of his death.
Modern fiction pairs him with Saitō Hajime as the corps' two great swords. The record says little about how the two men got on.
**In fiction** Okita's modern image was made in the 20th century. Shimozawa Kan's chronicles of the corps in the late 1920s collected memories and added colour; Shiba Ryōtarō's novels of the 1960s gave him the gentle, ironic, doomed character that films, television and later manga built on. By the end of the century he had become one of the most portrayed figures of the era — usually played by an actor much better-looking than the man his contemporaries described.
Legend & disputed: The image of Okita coughing blood at the Ikedaya comes from later fiction; Nagakura says only that he fell ill during the fight, and heatstroke is a common modern reading. The sandan-zuki, the black cat he failed to cut in his last days, and the beautiful face all belong to tradition or invention. Even his birth year is uncertain.

### Saitō Hajime [p:saito]
Role: Captain, 3rd unit · Life: 1844–1915
Captain of the third unit and the corps' most enigmatic figure — swordsman, instructor, and Kondō's spy inside Itō's breakaway group. He chose to stay and fight for Aizu rather than go north, survived its fall, and lived a second life as the Tokyo policeman Fujita Gorō, dying in 1915.
**Origins** Saitō was born in Edo in 1844 as Yamaguchi Hajime. His father had been a foot soldier of the Akashi domain who bought his way into the lowest rank of shogunal retainers. Almost nothing is known of his youth. A family tradition says that around 1862 he killed a man of hatamoto rank in a quarrel and fled to Kyoto, where he sheltered with a swordsman's family. Even his sword school is uncertain; sources name an Ittō-ryū lineage or Mugai-ryū. He seems to have known the men of the Shieikan before any of them went west.
**The third unit** He joined the newly formed corps in Kyoto in 1863, still in his teens, and was soon an assistant vice-commander. At the Ikedaya in 1864 he served in Hijikata's column; that autumn he was one of the six members who signed the complaint against Kondō to Aizu. When the corps was organised into units in 1865 he took the third, and he was one of its instructors in swordsmanship.
The corps used him for its darkest work. He is linked, on evidence of varying strength, to several of its internal killings. Whether or not each attribution is true, the leadership trusted him with matters it trusted to few others.
**The spy** In the spring of 1867 Saitō left with Itō Kashitarō's party to become one of the Goryō Eji, the guards of the late emperor's tomb. In late autumn he came back, carrying word that Itō meant to kill Kondō. Within weeks the corps had killed Itō and ambushed his followers at Aburanokōji. Saitō returned to duty under a new name, Yamaguchi Jirō, and a few weeks later commanded the corps' guard over the Kii official Miura Kyūtarō when Tosa men attacked them at the Tenmaya inn.
**For Aizu** Saitō fought at Toba–Fushimi and at Katsunuma, then went ahead to Aizu with the corps' wounded. With Hijikata injured, he commanded the corps at Shirakawa in the summer of 1868 and in the fighting that followed. When the leaders decided that autumn to leave Aizu and continue the war from the north, Saitō refused, reportedly saying that to abandon Aizu in its extremity would betray all that was right. A handful of men stayed with him. Attacked at Nyoraidō outside Wakamatsu, the group was scattered and reported wiped out, but Saitō survived and fought on until Aizu surrendered in November.
He went into captivity with the domain's men, and when the defeated domain was moved to Tonami, at the northern tip of Honshu, he went with it.
**Fujita Gorō** Saitō spent the rest of his life among Aizu people. Under the name Fujita Gorō he married Takagi Tokio, the daughter of an Aizu retainer, in 1874, with the domain's former lord Matsudaira Katamori standing as the senior go-between. That year he joined the new Tokyo police. In the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 he served with the police units sent to Kyushu and was wounded in action — fighting Satsuma once more, this time on the government's side.
After leaving the police he worked as a guard and clerk at the Tokyo Higher Normal School and then at the Women's Higher Normal School. He died in Tokyo on September 28, 1915, and is buried at Amida-ji in Aizu-Wakamatsu. Family tradition says he died seated formally in the alcove of his home.
**The silent survivor** Unlike Nagakura, Saitō left no memoir, gave no interviews and, as far as anyone knows, never told the corps' story to an outsider. What survives of his own voice comes from his family and a few acquaintances, and amounts to very little. He and Tokio had three sons; the eldest, Tsutomu, became an army officer. In Tokyo he was remembered as a tall, quiet man who drank, kept to himself, and went to Aizu gatherings.
That silence is why he has been reinvented so often. Novelists, film-makers and manga artists have made him a cynic, a killer, a sentimental loyalist and a master of a sword technique no one ever saw him use. The record supports only a few things firmly: that he served the corps in its most confidential work, that he chose Aizu over his own survival in 1868, and that he lived out his life among the people he had chosen.
**Comrades** Saitō's relationships are known mostly from what he did rather than what he said. Hijikata trusted him with the corps' most confidential work, and in 1868 the two men parted in Aizu, each convinced he had chosen the right duty. Kondō used him as his eyes inside Itō Kashitarō's breakaway group. That meant betraying Itō, who had accepted him as a follower, and Tōdō Heisuke, with whom he had served since the corps' first year and who died in the ambush Saitō's report set in motion.
In 1864 he had stood with Nagakura Shinpachi, Harada Sanosuke and Shimada Kai in the protest against Kondō's arrogance. Nagakura and he were among the last survivors of the corps' Kyoto officers, and died within nine months of each other in 1915.
His deepest tie in later life was to Aizu itself. Matsudaira Katamori acted as go-between at his marriage to Takagi Tokio, the daughter of an Aizu retainer, and Aizu's former leaders remained his friends and patrons in Tokyo. He chose to be buried among Aizu's dead.
Legend & disputed: The "left-handed thrust" associated with Saitō is fiction, popularised by 20th-century novels and manga; no contemporary source describes his technique. Treat detailed accounts of his killings within the corps with caution — he left almost no testimony of his own, which is exactly why fiction found him so irresistible. Whether he went into the Goryō Eji as a planted spy from the start is also unproven.

### Nagakura Shinpachi [p:nagakura]
Role: Captain, 2nd unit · Life: 1839–1915
Captain of the second unit and, by his peers' reckoning, perhaps the strongest pure swordsman in the corps. He fought through the Ikedaya at Kondō's side, told Kondō to his face when he thought him wrong, refused to become his retainer, and survived everything to become the Shinsengumi's great storyteller.
**A Matsumae swordsman** Nagakura was born on May 23, 1839, in the Edo residence of the Matsumae domain, the son of a retainer with a stipend of 150 koku — a respectable samurai house. Sword-mad from boyhood, he trained in Shindō Munen-ryū and earned his full licence in his late teens. Then, unwilling to spend his life at a desk, he left the domain's service to live for the sword — in effect deserting his hereditary place. He travelled from dōjō to dōjō to test himself, and settled as a guest at Kondō's Shieikan, where, he later wrote, the food was poor but the company suited him.
**Kyoto** He went west in the Rōshigumi in 1863, stayed in Kyoto with Kondō, and became an assistant vice-commander. He was in the party that brawled with the Osaka sumo wrestlers that summer. At the Ikedaya in July 1864 he was one of the handful who went in with Kondō and one of the few still fighting at the end: his sword was broken, his hand cut open, and his chain mail saved his life.
That autumn, disgusted by what he saw as Kondō's growing lordliness, he led five comrades in submitting a written complaint to Aizu, risking their lives on it. Katamori reconciled the two sides. It says much about the corps that Nagakura survived the protest and kept his rank: its core was still a band of sworn comrades, not a lord and his servants. In 1865 he took command of the second unit, and he fought in the street battle at Aburanokōji in 1867.
**The war and the break** Nagakura fought at Fushimi in January 1868 and in the rearguard at Katsunuma. Afterwards, when Kondō asked the survivors to serve him as his retainers, he refused: he had joined as Kondō's comrade, not his vassal. With Harada Sanosuke he left to form a new unit, the Seiheitai, and fought on against the new government in the north Kantō and towards Aizu. He never saw Kondō again.
**Sugimura Yoshie** Pardoned after the war, Nagakura returned to the service of the Matsumae domain and was adopted into the family of a domain physician, Sugimura, taking the name Sugimura Yoshie. In 1876, with the help of the physician Matsumoto Ryōjun, he raised the memorial at Itabashi to Kondō, Hijikata and the corps' dead. He later spent several years teaching swordsmanship to the guards of Kabato prison in Hokkaido, lived for a time in Tokyo, and settled in old age in Otaru.
He wrote his memories down more than once. A manuscript account of the corps' early years, lost for over a century, was rediscovered in 1998. In 1913 the Otaru Shimbun ran his recollections as a newspaper serial, and that story — vivid, partisan and indispensable — became the source of most of the corps' famous scenes. He died in Otaru on January 5, 1915, aged seventy-five, the same year as Saitō.
**A daughter in Kyoto** In Kyoto Nagakura had a companion, Kotsune, a woman of the Shimabara quarter, and with her a daughter, Iso, born around the end of the corps' Kyoto years. Kotsune died soon afterwards, and when Nagakura left with the corps the child was left in the care of others. By family accounts he looked for her for decades, and found her late in life: she had become an actress. It is one of the few stories of the corps' men that ends with something recovered rather than lost.
**Character** Nagakura was frank to the point of rudeness, loyal to his comrades rather than to rank, and proud of his sword to the end. He gave up a samurai's stipend to live as a wandering swordsman, joined Kondō as an equal, and never accepted being anyone's retainer. He liked drink, company and the pleasure quarters, and he was not above improving a story in the telling.
He was also, unusually for the corps, a man who looked back. He wrote down what had happened more than once, searched for decades for the daughter he had left in Kyoto, and spent his own money and effort on a memorial to men who had died as rebels. A family story has him as an old man, jostled by young toughs outside a theatre in Otaru, freezing them where they stood with a single look.
**Comrades** Nagakura's relationship with Kondō was the most complicated in the corps. He fought beside him at the Ikedaya, protested against his arrogance in 1864, refused to become his retainer in 1868 — and then, eight years after Kondō's execution, raised his memorial at Itabashi. Harada Sanosuke was his closest friend: they protested together, left Kondō together, and parted only when Harada turned back to Edo and his death at Ueno.
He had come to know Shimada Kai in Edo before either went west, and shared a sword school with Serizawa Kamo, whose party he rode with in Osaka and whose death he later described. His most famous memory concerns Tōdō Heisuke at Aburanokōji: by his account, Kondō had wanted Tōdō spared and Nagakura opened a way for him to escape, only for another member to cut him down. With the physician Matsumoto Ryōjun he worked to put up the Itabashi memorial, and in old age it was he, more than anyone, who kept the names of his comrades in circulation.
Legend & disputed: Nagakura's recollections are the backbone of Shinsengumi history and also its most coloured source: written decades later by a survivor with his own reputation to protect, they sometimes enlarge his part and soften his comrades' faults. His story of trying to let Tōdō Heisuke escape at Aburanokōji is a well-known example that cannot be checked.

### Harada Sanosuke [p:harada]
Role: Captain, 10th unit · Life: 1840–1868
Captain of the tenth unit and the corps' great spearman — hot-blooded, quick to laugh and quick to fight, with a scar across his belly from a seppuku attempt that he showed off at parties. One of the few members with a wife and child, he died at twenty-eight in a battle he had no obligation to join.
**The scar** Harada was born in 1840 in Matsuyama, in Iyo province on the island of Shikoku, and served the Matsuyama domain as a chūgen — an attendant, below samurai rank. The story he told all his life was that a samurai once mocked him as a man who did not even know how to cut his belly, and that the young Harada drew his blade and slashed his own abdomen on the spot to show him. He survived, and is said to have taken as his crest a circle with a single line through it, after the scar.
**Iyo Matsuyama** Matsuyama, the castle town where Harada grew up, was the seat of a branch of the Matsudaira — the Tokugawa's own kin — and its lords were among the shogunate's most dependable supporters. A chūgen was a servant with a short sword, carrying baggage and running errands for the domain's samurai: inside the samurai world, but at its bottom edge. Harada left that life behind, but he never lost the readiness to prove himself that the seppuku story shows.
**The spear** He left the domain, and in Osaka is said to have learned the spear from Tani Sanjūrō, later a fellow captain in the corps. Drifting east, he joined the circle of fighters around Kondō's Shieikan as a guest, and went west with them in the Rōshigumi in 1863.
**Kyoto** Harada fought in nearly every major action of the Kyoto years: the killing of Serizawa, the cordon outside the Ikedaya, the Kinmon fighting, the ambush at the Sanjō bridge in 1866, where his squad fell on Tosa men tearing down the shogunate's notice board, and the street battle at Aburanokōji. In 1864 he was one of the six who protested against Kondō to Aizu. From 1865 he commanded the tenth unit.
In Kyoto he married a local woman, Masa, and had a son, Shigeru, living with them outside the barracks with the leadership's permission — a settled domestic life that almost no one else in the corps attempted.
**Ryōma** When Sakamoto Ryōma and Nakaoka Shintarō were assassinated in Kyoto in December 1867, suspicion fell on the Shinsengumi, and on Harada in particular: a scabbard found at the scene was said to be his, and the dying Nakaoka was reported to have heard the attackers use a word of the Iyo dialect. The evidence was thin. Later testimony points to the Mimawarigumi, another shogunal police unit, as the killers.
**Death at Ueno** After Katsunuma in 1868, Harada left Kondō with Nagakura to form the Seiheitai. But on the march north he turned back towards Edo — some say to see his wife and son once more. There he joined the Shōgitai, the force of shogunal loyalists holding Ueno, and was badly wounded in the one-day Battle of Ueno on July 4, 1868. He died two days later, aged twenty-eight. Masa and Shigeru, far away in Kyoto, learned of it only later.
**Masa** Masa outlived her husband by more than sixty years. She raised their son in Kyoto, and in old age her memories of Harada were recorded by the writer Shimozawa Kan: a cheerful, affectionate man who came home from the barracks to play with the baby, and who left for the east in 1868 promising that he would send for them. Hers is one of the very few accounts of a Shinsengumi man at home rather than at work.
**Character** Everyone who wrote about Harada remembered the same man: loud, cheerful, quick-tempered, fond of drink and of a fight, and generous. He showed off his scar, told and retold the story of how he got it, and charged into every fight the corps had. He was also almost the only officer who built a family in Kyoto and went home to it at night. That combination — the brawler who played with his baby son — is what made his early death so widely mourned.
**Comrades** Nagakura Shinpachi was his closest friend. They came to the Shieikan as outsiders from other schools, protested together against Kondō's arrogance in 1864, and walked away from Kondō together in 1868; they parted only when Harada turned back towards Edo. To Kondō and Hijikata he was a loyal and reliable fighter, if never an intimate. Tani Sanjūrō, by tradition his teacher of the spear in Osaka, became a fellow captain.
His name was linked by suspicion, falsely, to the killing of Sakamoto Ryōma, and the men of Tosa did not forget it. He was by most accounts one of the men who killed Serizawa Kamo, with whom he had served in the corps' first months. And Masa is one of the very few partners in the corps' story who left her own account of the man she married.
Legend & disputed: A persistent legend holds that Harada survived Ueno, crossed to the continent and reappeared decades later as a mounted bandit chief in Manchuria. It is romance, not history — but it says something about how little anyone wanted this particular man to be dead. The seppuku story, the crest, his training under Tani and his joining the Shōgitai all rest largely on later accounts.

### Inoue Genzaburō [p:inoue]
Role: Captain, 6th unit · Life: 1829–1868
The quiet elder of the Shieikan circle. A Hino man from a family of part-time shogunal guards, he spent more than a decade earning a sword licence that came easily to younger men, followed Kondō and Hijikata to Kyoto as their senior, and was killed at Yodo in the first days of the Boshin War — the first of the original Tama comrades to die in battle.
**A Hino family of guards** Inoue was born in 1829 in Hino, the post town on the Kōshū road a few kilometres from Hijikata's village of Ishida. His family belonged to the Hachiōji Thousand-Man Guard (Hachiōji sennin dōshin), a peculiar institution of the Tama region: some thousand households of farmers who held minor shogunal rank, kept their swords and their fields, and took turns on guard duty, most famously at the Tokugawa shrines at Nikkō. They were not quite samurai and not quite peasants — exactly the in-between class that would later fill the Shinsengumi's ranks.
He was a younger son, with no claim on the family's position. His elder brother Matsugorō inherited it, and the brothers stayed close; Matsugorō's later journey to Kyoto would leave one of the few eyewitness glimpses of the corps' first months.
**The Shieikan's elder** As a youth Inoue entered the Tennen Rishin-ryū under Kondō Shūsuke, the master who would adopt Kondō Isami. The school's real base was not its small Edo dojo but the farming houses of Tama, where Shūsuke and his successors gave lessons on circuit; the Hino headman Satō Hikogorō kept a practice hall in his own home. Inoue trained there and in Edo for well over a decade before receiving his full licence, around 1860 — slowly, by the standards of younger men like Okita.
That slowness became part of his character in later memory: not a gifted swordsman but a dependable one, older than Kondō and Hijikata, quietly loyal, the one who kept the house running. In the Shieikan's crowded, rowdy years he was something like an elder brother to the younger men, and tradition — affectionate, if hard to document — remembers him as the steady presence everyone took for granted.
**Kyoto** In early 1863 Inoue marched west with the Rōshigumi and was one of the Shieikan men who stayed behind in Kyoto with Kondō when the rest returned to Edo. In the new corps he served as an assistant to the vice-commander (fukuchō jokin), the band of officers between the leadership and the rank and file.
On the night of the Ikedaya incident in July 1864 he was in Hijikata's column, which searched the riverside inns before converging on the Ikedaya to seal the area. When the corps was reorganized into numbered units in 1865, he took command of the 6th unit. In 1867 he was among the officers made direct retainers of the shogunate. He was never one of the corps' famous swordsmen or schemers; his role was the less visible work of an officer who could be relied on.
**Death at Yodo** When war came in January 1868, the corps fought first at Fushimi and then fell back south with the retreating shogunal army. On January 29, 1868, in the fighting along the Yodo river embankment at Senryōmatsu, Inoue was shot and killed. He was about forty.
His nephew Inoue Taisuke, a boy of about twelve serving with the corps, tried to carry his uncle's head and sword away from the field so they could be taken home. The weight was too much for him in the rout; he is said to have buried them near a temple on the way, at a spot that has never been identified. In Hino, the family raised a memorial at Hōsen-ji, and his descendants still keep his memory in a small family museum near the old post town.
**Character** Inoue was the steady one: older than Kondō and Hijikata, slow and patient, not a brilliant swordsman but a dependable one, loyal without needing to be noticed. In a house full of prodigies and egos he was something like an elder brother, the man who kept things running and whom everyone took for granted until he was gone. Little in the record suggests ambition. He went to Kyoto because the people he had trained with since boyhood were going.
**Family and comrades** His family were the core of his world. His elder brother Matsugorō, who inherited the family's place in the Hachiōji guard, travelled to Kyoto in 1863 in the shogun's escort and left a diary with one of the few eyewitness glimpses of the corps' first months; his nephew Taisuke followed him into the corps as a boy, and tried to carry his head home from Senryōmatsu.
Hijikata, from the neighbouring village of Ishida, had known him since boyhood. Kondō and Okita had grown up with him in the school; in the corps he served under the men he had once trained beside, without any sign of resentment. Of the Shieikan circle, he was the first to die in battle, and his death at Yodo hit Hijikata, who was commanding that day, particularly hard.
Legend & disputed: The story of Taisuke and his uncle's head comes down through the Inoue family and later retellings; its outline is consistent, but details such as the exact temple vary and cannot be checked. So does the popular picture of Inoue as a plodding swordsman: his late licence is documented, but "slow" may say more about the prodigies around him than about him.

### Yamanami Keisuke [p:yamanami]
Role: General secretary (sōchō) · Life: 1833–1865
The corps' gentle, scholarly general secretary — a licensed Hokushin Ittō-ryū swordsman whom the people of Mibu called "Yamanami the Buddha." Increasingly at odds with the corps' direction, he left it, came back without resistance when he was overtaken, and died by its code, mourned even by those who enforced it.
**Origins** Yamanami's early life is thinly documented. By tradition he was born in 1833 into a retainer family of the Sendai domain in the north. He trained in Hokushin Ittō-ryū, the polished, modern sword school of the Chiba family in Edo, and was also well read — a man who could argue a point of scholarship as well as fence. One story has him losing a bout to Kondō and, as wandering swordsmen sometimes did, attaching himself to the man who had beaten him. By the early 1860s he was a guest at the Shieikan, the even-tempered elder among its fighters.
**Vice-commander** He went west with Kondō's household in 1863 and in the corps' first months held the title of vice-commander alongside Hijikata. He is named in several accounts among the men who killed Serizawa Kamo that autumn — the gentle man's darkest night. In the reorganisation that followed he was given the grand title of general secretary, ranking beside Hijikata, but real authority over the corps' running flowed through Hijikata, and Yamanami was steadily sidelined. By one account he was wounded in the arm in a fight with robbers in Osaka and fought less after that.
**At odds** When Itō Kashitarō arrived in late 1864, Yamanami found a kindred spirit: a fellow Hokushin Ittō-ryū man and a scholar who shared his loyalty to the imperial cause. His unease with the corps seems to have been more than personal. Sources suggest that he opposed its hardening character, and by some accounts the plan to move the barracks into Nishi Honganji, the head temple of a sect with millions of common believers.
**Leaving and returning** In March 1865 Yamanami left a note and rode east — desertion under the corps' code, which he had helped to establish, and punishable by death. The man sent after him was Okita Sōji, who had grown up beside him at the Shieikan. At Ōtsu, the first post town on the road east, Okita caught up with him, and Yamanami came back without resistance, though escape was surely within his power.
On March 20, 1865, he died by seppuku at the corps' barracks in the Maekawa house at Mibu, with Okita as his second. He was thirty-one or thirty-two. Itō Kashitarō wrote poems in his memory. The people of Mibu, whose children he had befriended, mourned him openly, and his grave at the temple of Kōen-ji in Mibu still receives flowers.
**Remembered at Mibu** Yamanami is buried at Kōen-ji, a small temple in Mibu that became the corps' burial place for members who died in its Kyoto years. His name is on the temple's register of the dead, and for a century and a half people have come to leave flowers at his grave. Of all the corps' leaders, he is the one the townspeople of Mibu remembered with the most affection, and the one whose death the corps itself seems most to have regretted.
**Character** Yamanami is remembered above all as kind. The people of Mibu called him "the Buddha"; the Yagi and Maekawa children remembered a man who played with them. He was educated, even-tempered and a natural mediator, the man in Kondō's household most likely to calm a quarrel or to argue a point from a book. He was also a licensed swordsman of a demanding school, and he did not shrink from the corps' violence in its first year. What he could not accept, it seems, was the corps that violence was turning into.
**Comrades** Kondō, who had beaten him in a bout and won his allegiance, valued him as an educated equal. With Hijikata, his fellow vice-commander and opposite in temperament, relations were correct but distant; as Hijikata's power grew, Yamanami's shrank. Okita Sōji was close to him, almost a younger brother — which is why it was Okita who was sent after him, and Okita who served as his second.
Itō Kashitarō, who arrived in late 1864, was a kindred spirit: a fellow Hokushin Ittō-ryū man and a scholar of the imperial cause. Itō wrote poems mourning him. Two years later Itō would take the exit from the corps that Yamanami had not found. The courtesan Akesato, said to have been his lover, belongs to legend more than to record.
Legend & disputed: Why he left, and why he came back, are the corps' most haunting questions; no letter or statement of his survives to answer them. The farewell with his lover Akesato of Shimabara through the lattice window of the barracks first appears in Shimozawa Kan's 20th-century chronicles, which blended interviews with invention. His birth year, his Sendai origins and the Osaka wound all rest on tradition.

### Serizawa Kamo [p:serizawa]
Role: Founding commander · Life: c.1826–1863
The corps' violent founding commander: a country samurai from Mito, steeped in its radical loyalism, educated and proud, and a dangerous drunk. He shared command uneasily with Kondō's farmers' sons, led the corps at its first great moment, and shamed its Aizu patrons until his own comrades were ordered to kill him.
**Where he came from** Serizawa came from the village of Serizawa in the Namekata district of Hitachi province, near Lake Kasumigaura — today part of Namegata, Ibaraki. His family were gōshi: country samurai who farmed their own land but held samurai status under the Mito domain, took their name from the village, and traced themselves to a medieval warrior house of the district. He was born around 1826, by most accounts a younger son. Like many younger sons of such houses he had to make his own way, and by tradition he was for a time adopted into another family under the name Shimomura Tsuguji. His birth year, early name and family details are all disputed.
Mito was no ordinary domain. Its scholars had written the doctrine of reverence for the emperor that was now inflaming the country, and its young men had killed the shogunate's chief minister at the Sakurada Gate in 1860. Serizawa grew up inside that world. He trained in Shintō Munen-ryū, the sword school favoured by Mito's activists — the same school as Nagakura Shinpachi — and by tradition held its full licence.
**The Mito radical** In the early 1860s Mito's radicals broke into armed bands that roamed the Kantō demanding "contributions" from merchants for the cause of expelling the foreigners. Serizawa is generally identified with one of the leaders of such a band. The domain cracked down; he was imprisoned and condemned to death, and a story told later has him biting his finger and writing a death poem in blood in his cell. In 1862, as the shogunate tried to win back the loyalists, a general amnesty set him free. Months later Kiyokawa Hachirō's call for masterless swordsmen gave him a new stage.
**The Rōshigumi** He joined the Rōshigumi in 1863 as the leader of a small Mito group — Niimi Nishiki, Hirayama Gorō, Hirama Jūsuke, Noguchi Kenji and a few others. In Kyoto his group and Kondō's were the two that refused to go back east with Kiyokawa, and under Aizu's protection Serizawa became the senior of the corps' three commanders, with Niimi and Kondō. He carried a great iron-ribbed war fan inscribed "a man of utmost loyalty and patriotism."
**The Mibu months** In Kyoto, Serizawa's conduct quickly became the corps' liability. He forced loans from merchants; when the silk merchant Yamatoya refused, he had its storehouse burned in the middle of the city. He led the brawl in Osaka in which sumo wrestlers were killed, and by tradition wrecked the rooms of the Sumiya, the great house of entertainment in Shimabara, when he was displeased with its service. Each outrage reached the Aizu domain, the corps' patron and guarantor.
Yet he also led the corps at its first great moment, the coup of September 1863 that drove Chōshū from Kyoto, when, by a well-known anecdote, he faced down the Aizu guards who barred the way with nothing but his iron fan.
**Character** Serizawa was not simply a brute. He was educated, wrote a good hand and composed verse, and carried himself as a samurai of standing among men who mostly were not. He could be generous and expansive, and he had the self-assurance of someone who had faced a death sentence for his beliefs and walked out of prison. He was also a heavy drinker who turned violent when drunk, and many of the outrages laid at his door came at the end of a night's drinking.
The Yagi family, on whose house the corps was billeted and who saw him every day, remembered both men: one who could be courteous with the household and easy with its children when sober, and one who smashed things and threatened people when he was not. His iron fan sums him up — a radical's slogan, carried as a weapon.
**Kondō and Hijikata** Relations with Kondō's group were uneasy from the start. Serizawa had samurai birth, Mito credentials and seniority; Kondō's men were farmers' sons from a provincial sword school. On the march west, by Nagakura's account, Serizawa answered a slight over lodgings with a bonfire in the street at Honjō, and Kondō, whose mistake it had been, apologised.
In Kyoto the two groups needed each other — Aizu took them on together — and for some months Kondō deferred to the older man. But the corps Hijikata was building, with its code and its discipline, had no room for a commander who broke every rule it made. When Aizu's patience ran out, it was Hijikata, by most accounts, who led the men who killed Serizawa, and Kondō who gained from it.
**His own men** Serizawa's faction were Mito men who had come west with him. Niimi Nishiki, his right hand and a fellow commander, was the first to go: confronted in a Gion teahouse with charges of misconduct, he was forced to commit seppuku days before Serizawa was killed. Hirayama Gorō died beside Serizawa that night. Hirama Jūsuke, sleeping elsewhere in the house, got away and was never caught. Noguchi Kenji, the youngest, survived the purge only to be ordered to commit seppuku a few months later. Within half a year of Serizawa's death, his faction had ceased to exist.
**The others** Nagakura Shinpachi, who shared Serizawa's sword school, was with him in Osaka at the sumo brawl, and his memoir is one of the main sources for Serizawa's Kyoto months — the outrages and the killing alike. He was not among the men named as the killers. Okita Sōji, Yamanami Keisuke and Harada Sanosuke drank with Serizawa and served under him, and by most accounts were among those who came for him in the night.
Oume, the mistress of a cloth merchant who had come to the Yagi house to press for a debt Serizawa owed, became his lover, and died with him. To the Aizu domain he was useful and an embarrassment at once: he had led the corps at the palace gates in September, and he was burning merchants' storehouses in the city it was meant to protect. In the end Aizu decided he was more embarrassment than use.
**Assassination** On a night of driving rain in the autumn of 1863, after a party at the Sumiya, Serizawa was cut down as he slept at the Yagi house in Mibu. The corps blamed Chōshū assassins and gave him a grand funeral. With his death, command passed undivided to Kondō, and the Shinsengumi as history knows it began.
**Grave** Serizawa and Hirayama Gorō are buried together at Mibu-dera, the temple next to the corps' first barracks, where the corps' members once drilled and where the Mibu people's spring kyōgen plays are still performed. For years the corps' official story — that Chōshū assassins had killed them — went unchallenged in public; the truth came out only when the survivors began to talk.
Legend & disputed: Serizawa is history's designated villain, and much of the detail comes from the winning faction and from the Yagi family's later reminiscences. The outrages are well attested; the cartoonish brute is partly narrative convenience. Some historians read him as a genuine radical who saw the corps as his movement, not Kondō's. His identification with the imprisoned Mito activist, the adoption into the Shimomura family, the blood-written death poem, the Sumiya rampage and the fan at the palace gate are all tradition, and the exact date of his death differs between accounts.

### Tōdō Heisuke [p:todo]
Role: Captain, 8th unit · Life: 1844–1867
The youngest of the original Shieikan circle, nicknamed "Sakigake-sensei" — Mr. First-In — for always charging first. He took a sword cut across the forehead at the Ikedaya, brought Itō Kashitarō into the corps, then left with him, and was killed at Aburanokōji fighting the men he had marched beside for four years.
**Origins** Tōdō was born in Edo in 1844. He claimed — or it was claimed for him — to be an unacknowledged son of the lord of the Tsu domain, whose family name he shared; the story was never proven, and it may have been no more than a way for a young man of uncertain birth to stand a little taller among swordsmen. What is clearer is that he trained in Hokushin Ittō-ryū, the polished Edo school of the Chiba family, before drifting into the rough company of Kondō's Shieikan.
He was barely nineteen when the Rōshigumi marched west in 1863, the youngest of the men who would form the core of the Shinsengumi.
**First through the door** In Kyoto Tōdō served as an assistant vice-commander and earned his nickname by always being first into a fight. At the Ikedaya in July 1864 he was one of the handful of men who went in with Kondō. In the stifling heat he pushed back his protective headband, and a sword stroke opened his forehead; he fought on half-blinded by blood and was carried out with the wound that marked him for the rest of his short life.
That autumn he went ahead to Edo to prepare a recruiting drive, and through his old ties in Hokushin Ittō-ryū circles he brought Kondō an important catch: Itō Kashitarō, a learned swordsman with his own following. When the corps was reorganized in 1865, Tōdō took command of the 8th unit.
**With Itō** Itō's arrival changed the corps. He was a scholar of the imperial cause, and his views drifted steadily away from Kondō's loyalty to the shogunate. In the spring of 1867 Itō left to found the Goryō Eji, guards of the late Emperor Kōmei's tomb, taking more than a dozen men with him. Tōdō went with him. Whether he went from conviction, from loyalty to the man he had recruited, or from old ties to their shared sword school, no one can now say.
**Aburanokōji** On December 13, 1867, Itō was invited to a drinking party at Kondō's mistress's house, cut down on his way home, and his body left in the street at Aburanokōji as bait. When the Goryō Eji came to recover it, the Shinsengumi was waiting. Tōdō died in the fight that followed, at twenty-three. He is buried with Itō and the other fallen of the Goryō Eji at Kaikō-ji, a sub-temple of Sennyū-ji in eastern Kyoto.
**Character** Tōdō was young, quick and fearless — the corps called him the "vanguard" because he was always first through the door — and he was by most accounts well liked. He had the polish of the Chiba family's Edo school and the restlessness of a young man of uncertain birth looking for a place to stand. He was also more open to ideas than most of his comrades: the cause of the emperor that Itō Kashitarō preached clearly moved him.
**Comrades** Tōdō was the youngest of Kondō's Shieikan circle, and the one whose loyalties were divided in the end. He brought Itō Kashitarō into the corps through their shared sword school, and when Itō left in 1867 Tōdō went with him — whether from conviction, from loyalty to the man he had recruited, or from old ties of school, no one can now say. Yamanami Keisuke, another Hokushin Ittō-ryū man, was close to both of them.
On the other side stood the men he had grown up with. Kondō, by Nagakura's account, wanted him spared at Aburanokōji, and Nagakura says he opened a way for Tōdō to escape. Saitō Hajime, who had gone into the Goryō Eji beside him, had been reporting on them all along. Tōdō died at twenty-three among the men he had chosen, at the hands of the men he had left.
Legend & disputed: Nagakura Shinpachi later wrote that Kondō wanted Tōdō spared, and that Nagakura himself opened a way for him to escape — only for another member, not knowing the order, to cut him down from behind. It is a moving story, told by a man who was there, but it comes from a memoir written decades later and cannot be checked. The Tsu-domain parentage is likewise unproven.

### Takeda Kanryūsai [p:takeda]
Role: Captain, 5th unit; military instructor · Life: ?–1867
The corps' instructor in traditional military science and one of its most ambitious officers. He fought at the Ikedaya and rose to captain the 5th unit, but when the corps turned to Western drill his learning became obsolete, his standing collapsed, and he left — and was killed by the corps in 1867.
**A military scholar** Takeda came from Izumo province, on the Sea of Japan coast, and made his way as a teacher of Kōshū-style military science — the classical Japanese art of armies, formations and strategy, descended from the campaigns of the Takeda clan of Kai. He joined the corps in Kyoto in 1863, after the Rōshigumi had split, and his learning gave him a place few rank-and-file swordsmen could claim: he became the corps' instructor in military science, and later also taught letters. In a corps of swordsmen, he was the man who could talk about armies.
**Rise** He was one of the handful of men who went into the Ikedaya with Kondō in July 1864 and was rewarded accordingly. When the corps was reorganized in 1865 he was given the 5th unit. Contemporaries and later writers alike portray him as an assiduous courtier of Kondō's favour, and he seems to have made enemies among men who valued plain dealing.
**Obsolete** From 1865 the corps began training in Western-style drill and firearms, and the shogunate itself was rebuilding its army on French lines. Kōshū-style military science, Takeda's whole claim to rank, became a relic almost overnight. His influence waned; he is said to have sought new patrons outside the corps, including, by some accounts, among its enemies. Sometime in 1866 or early 1867 he left.
In the summer of 1867 he was killed on the bank of the Kamo river near the Zenitori bridge, by men of the corps sent after him. For an officer who had left and was suspected of dealing with the other side, the Shinsengumi's code allowed only one outcome.
**Character** Takeda is one of the few members whom almost every account dislikes. Nagakura and later writers portray him as a flatterer who cultivated Kondō's favour and looked down on plain fighters, and as a man more interested in his own rank than in the corps. Some of that may be the verdict of men who resented a scholar promoted over them, and of a corps that later killed him and needed to explain why. But he was clearly ambitious, clearly clever, and clearly unable to adapt when the knowledge that had made him valuable went out of date.
**Comrades** Kondō was his patron, and Takeda's rise depended on his favour. He went into the Ikedaya at Kondō's side with Okita, Nagakura and Tōdō, the brightest moment of his career. When Itō Kashitarō arrived in 1864, a better-qualified scholar took the place in Kondō's esteem that Takeda had occupied, and the move to Western drill took away the rest. By some accounts he then looked for new patrons outside the corps, including among Satsuma men. Popular tradition names Saitō Hajime as the man sent to kill him; the record does not.
Legend & disputed: Which members killed Takeda is not recorded with certainty; popular accounts name Saitō Hajime, but that rests on later tradition. Later fiction also made much of his personal life, with little basis in the record. His exact date of birth is unknown.

### Tani Sanjūrō [p:tani]
Role: Captain, 7th unit; spear instructor · Life: ?–1866
A spearman from a samurai family of Bitchū Matsuyama who joined with his two younger brothers. He captained the 7th unit and taught the spear; his youngest brother was adopted by Kondō as heir. He died suddenly in Gion in 1866, and how he died is still argued over.
**Three brothers** Tani came from a samurai family of the Bitchū Matsuyama domain, in what is now Okayama prefecture. The family lost its position and the brothers made their living by their skills; Sanjūrō and his brother Mantarō were spearmen, and Mantarō ran a dōjō in Osaka. The three brothers — Sanjūrō, Mantarō and the youngest, Masatake — all joined the Shinsengumi in its Kyoto years.
Their home domain had an unusual connection to the regime the corps served: its lord, Itakura Katsukiyo, was one of the shogunate's senior councillors (rōjū) through much of the 1860s. Whether that tie helped the brothers find a place in a shogunal police corps is not recorded, but men from Matsuyama had reason to see the shogunate's cause as their own.
**Captain and kinsman** Sanjūrō's standing rose quickly. Around this time the corps formalised its training by appointing instructors in each discipline — swordsmanship, spear, jūjutsu, military science, letters, gunnery and horsemanship — so that a captain was often also a teacher. When the corps was reorganized in 1865 Tani received the 7th unit and served as its instructor in the spear, the weapon of choice for holding a narrow street or doorway. That year Kondō, who had no son, adopted the youngest brother Masatake as his heir under the name Kondō Shūhei — a remarkable honour that bound the Tani family to the commander himself.
**Death in Gion** In the spring of 1866 Tani died suddenly near the stone steps of the Yasaka shrine in Gion. The records of the corps do not explain how. After his death, the Tani family's place close to Kondō did not last; Shūhei's adoption was later dissolved.
**Character and comrades** Tani carried himself as a samurai of standing, and his family's sudden closeness to Kondō — through the adoption of his youngest brother as Kondō's heir — seems to have caused resentment among men who had been with Kondō from the start. Later writers made him arrogant and incompetent, though little contemporary evidence supports either charge.
His world was his family. His brother Mantarō fought inside the Ikedaya with Kondō while Sanjūrō searched the riverside with Hijikata; his youngest brother Masatake became, for a time, Kondō Shūhei, the commander's adopted son. By tradition Harada Sanosuke had learned the spear from him in Osaka. His domain's lord, Itakura Katsukiyo, was one of the shogunate's senior councillors. After his death the family's place near Kondō faded, and the adoption was dissolved.
Legend & disputed: Because his death was sudden and unexplained, rumour filled the gap. Popular accounts say he was assassinated by his own comrades, and Saitō Hajime is often named — but no contemporary record supports this, and illness remains just as possible. Stories that he was disliked and incompetent come largely from later writers.

### Matsubara Chūji [p:matsubara]
Role: Captain, 4th unit; jūjutsu instructor · Life: ?–1865
A big, shaven-headed jūjutsu master from Osaka, known for his kindness, who captained the 4th unit. He died in 1865, and the circumstances are lost under one of the most enduring legends of the corps.
**The "modern Benkei"** Matsubara was a master of Sekiguchi-ryū jūjutsu from the Osaka area who joined the corps in its early Kyoto days. Sekiguchi-ryū was one of the oldest schools of jūjutsu, founded in the early Edo period and taught across western Japan — a respectable pedigree for a fighter whose art relied on throws, locks and close grappling rather than the sword. A large man who kept his head shaved, he was nicknamed after Benkei, the giant warrior-monk of medieval legend. He was remembered as good-natured and generous — in a corps known for its severity, a man people liked.
**Captain** When the corps was reorganized in 1865, Matsubara was given command of the 4th unit and served as the corps' instructor in jūjutsu, the art of fighting unarmed or close in. In these years the corps formalised its training by appointing instructors in each discipline — swordsmanship, spear, jūjutsu, military science, letters, gunnery and horsemanship — so that a captain was often also a teacher. Beyond these appointments, the record of Matsubara's service is thin.
**Death** Matsubara died in the autumn of 1865. Contemporary records note the death but say little about its cause, and some accounts suggest he had fallen from favour shortly before. Into that silence stepped one of the corps' most famous stories.
**Character and comrades** Matsubara is one of the few members remembered simply as likeable: a big, shaven-headed man nicknamed after the warrior-monk Benkei, good-natured and generous, in a corps known for its severity. Of his relationships almost nothing is recorded. He came from the Osaka region, like Yamazaki Susumu and the Tani brothers, part of the Kansai contingent that joined the Tama men in the corps' first year. At the Ikedaya he searched the riverside inns in Hijikata's column. Then, within months of receiving his unit, he was gone — and the silence about why is the most telling thing about him.
Legend & disputed: The "Mibu double suicide" story — that Matsubara killed a man in a quarrel, fell in love with his widow while caring for her, and died with her — was popularised by Shimozawa Kan in the 20th century. It is a powerful tale, but it has no support in contemporary records and should be read as fiction built around a real man's unexplained death.

### Suzuki Mikisaburō [p:suzuki]
Role: Captain, 9th unit · Life: 1837–1919
Itō Kashitarō's younger brother, who joined with him in 1864 and captained the 9th unit. He left with Itō for the Goryō Eji, survived the ambush at Aburanokōji that killed his brother, fought against the shogunate in the Boshin War, and lived on into the 20th century.
**Itō's brother** Suzuki was born in 1837 in Shizuku, Hitachi province, the younger brother of the man who would become Itō Kashitarō; the family name was Suzuki, and Itō took his new name on being adopted into a dōjō family in Edo. When Itō joined the Shinsengumi in the autumn of 1864, Mikisaburō came with him.
**Captain** In the 1865 reorganization he received the 9th unit. His position owed a great deal to his brother, whose learning and following made him one of the most important men in the corps; Mikisaburō's own record in these years is modest.
**The Goryō Eji and after** In 1867 he left with Itō to form the Goryō Eji. On the night of December 13, 1867, when the survivors came to recover Itō's body at Aburanokōji and were ambushed, Mikisaburō was among those who escaped; he found refuge in the Satsuma domain's residence. The survivors of the Goryō Eji took their revenge where they could — Kondō was shot and wounded from ambush a few weeks later — and in the Boshin War that followed they fought on the side of the new government.
Suzuki lived until 1919, long enough to see the Shinsengumi pass from living memory into legend.
**Character** Suzuki lived in his brother's shadow. Itō was the scholar, the speaker and the leader; Mikisaburō was the steady younger brother who followed him from Hitachi to Edo, from Edo into the corps, and out of it again. Little of his own character comes through the record, which is itself telling: he was the kind of man who is in the room for every turning point and is rarely the one quoted. He was also the one who lasted. Of Itō's party at Aburanokōji he lived longest, into his eighties, long enough to see the corps that killed his brother become a national legend.
**Comrades** His life was bound to his brother's. With Itō came Shinohara Taishinosuke and others of their Edo circle, and with them Suzuki served in the corps, left it for the Goryō Eji, and survived the ambush at Aburanokōji, escaping with Shinohara to the protection of the Satsuma residence. In the war that followed, the survivors fought on the side of the new government against the men who had been their comrades. Tōdō Heisuke, who had brought his brother into the corps, died beside his brother's body that night.

### Itō Kashitarō [p:ito]
Role: Staff officer (sanbō) · Life: 1835–1867
The most learned man ever to join the Shinsengumi: a Mito-trained scholar and master swordsman who brought his own following into the corps in 1864, rose at once to staff officer, and within three years had split it — founding the imperial tomb guards known as the Goryō Eji. He was lured to a party by Kondō and cut down on the way home at Aburanokōji.
**Scholar and swordsman** Itō was born in 1835 in Shizuku, Hitachi province (now part of Kasumigaura in Ibaraki), as Suzuki Ōkura, the son of a minor retainer whose family fell on hard times. Hitachi lay next to the Mito domain, the intellectual heart of the sonnō-jōi movement, and Itō absorbed its learning — the study of Japanese history as a story of loyalty to the emperor — alongside its swordsmanship.
He went to Edo and trained in Hokushin Ittō-ryū at the Fukagawa dōjō of Itō Seiichi. He did so well that the family adopted him as heir; he married into it, took the name Itō, and inherited the school. By his late twenties he was a dōjō master with his own students, a published poet and a man of political convictions — an unusual combination in an age when most swordsmen were one thing or the other.
**Into the corps** In the autumn of 1864, after the Ikedaya had made the Shinsengumi famous, Kondō went east to recruit. Tōdō Heisuke, who had trained in the same school, brought him to Itō. Itō agreed to join and came west with his younger brother Suzuki Mikisaburō and several of his own followers. Because the year was a kinoe-ne (甲子) year in the sixty-year calendar, he took the name Kashitarō to mark it.
Kondō gave him an extraordinary welcome: the new post of staff officer (sanbō), outranking the unit captains, and the instructorship in letters. In 1865 and 1866 Itō went with Kondō on the shogunate's missions to Hiroshima to question envoys of Chōshū — work that called for learning and argument, not swordsmanship.
**The split** Itō's loyalties were not Kondō's. Kondō served the shogunate; Itō, the Mito scholar, served the emperor, and by 1866 the two causes were visibly pulling apart. Rather than desert — which the corps punished with death — Itō found an honourable exit. In the spring of 1867, with the backing of a court-connected temple, he and more than a dozen men left to become the Goryō Eji, guards of the tomb of the late Emperor Kōmei. Based at a sub-temple of Kōdai-ji in eastern Kyoto, they were also called the Kōdai-ji party.
Among those who followed him were Tōdō Heisuke and — as the corps would later reveal — Saitō Hajime, who was in fact Kondō's spy. In the months that followed, Itō petitioned the court with proposals for national policy and moved in loyalist circles that the Shinsengumi had been formed to suppress.
**Aburanokōji** Saitō returned to the corps in the late autumn of 1867 with the report that Itō was plotting to kill Kondō. On December 13, 1867, Kondō invited Itō to drink at his mistress's house. Itō went — whether from confidence, courtesy or a misjudgment of his old comrades. On his way home, near Aburanokōji, he was speared and cut down. His body was left in the street to draw out the Goryō Eji, and when they came to recover it, the Shinsengumi was waiting.
Itō was thirty-two. He is buried with Tōdō, Hattori and the other dead of that night at Kaikō-ji, a sub-temple of Sennyū-ji, near the imperial tombs he had sworn to guard.
**Character** Itō was everything the Shieikan men were not: a trained scholar, a published poet, a polished speaker and a man with a political programme. Contemporaries describe him as handsome, courteous and persuasive, and he drew devoted followers wherever he went. He was also ambitious. He came to Kyoto as Kondō's guest and senior officer, and within two years was leading his own party and petitioning the court in his own name.
Whether he was a principled loyalist who tried to leave the corps honourably, or a schemer who used it as a stepping-stone and planned to destroy it, is the central question of his life. The evidence for the first is his writing; the evidence for the second comes almost entirely from the men who killed him.
**Comrades and enemies** Kondō recruited him, honoured him with rank above the captains, and took him on missions to Hiroshima; their alliance turned to rivalry as their loyalties pulled apart, and ended at Aburanokōji. Hijikata, by most accounts, distrusted him from the start. Tōdō Heisuke, who had brought him into the corps through their shared sword school, followed him out of it and died with him. Yamanami Keisuke was a kindred spirit, and Itō mourned him in verse.
His own people were his younger brother Suzuki Mikisaburō, his chief lieutenant Shinohara Taishinosuke, the two-sword fighter Hattori Takeo, and Kanō Michinosuke — the man who, months after Itō's death, recognised Kondō at Itabashi. Saitō Hajime joined them as a follower and reported on them to Kondō. In his last months Itō moved in loyalist circles; by one tradition he even called on Sakamoto Ryōma days before Ryōma's assassination to warn him that he was in danger.
Legend & disputed: Whether Itō really planned to assassinate Kondō is unproven; the claim comes from the Shinsengumi side, through Saitō, and served to justify the killing. His famous dying words — "Traitors!" — come from later retellings. Many historians see him less as a schemer than as a man of real convictions who tried, and failed, to leave the corps without breaking its law.

### Shinohara Tainoshin [p:shinohara]
Role: Inspector, then Goryō Eji · Life: 1828–1911
Itō's right-hand man: a tough, older fighter from Kyushu who joined with him in 1864, served as an inspector and jūjutsu instructor, left with him for the Goryō Eji, survived the night Itō was killed — and lived on into the 20th century, leaving one of the few accounts written from the other side of the corps' great split.
**From Kurume to Edo** Shinohara was born in 1828 in Chikugo province in northern Kyushu, in the territory of the Kurume domain, of humble origins. Kurume was a hotbed of loyalist feeling — the priest Maki Izumi, who would die at Tennōzan after the Kinmon battle, came from there — and Shinohara grew up within reach of that world. He made his way to Edo, served in the households of samurai, and became a capable fighter with the spear and in jūjutsu. Somewhere along the way he fell in with Itō Kashitarō's circle, and when Itō joined the Shinsengumi in 1864, Shinohara — seven years older and a man of the world — came with him.
**In the corps** In the Shinsengumi he served as an inspector and investigator — the officers who watched the members and gathered intelligence in the city — and as one of the corps' jūjutsu instructors. He was Itō's most dependable lieutenant, and in 1867 he went with him to found the Goryō Eji.
**Survivor** On the night of December 13, 1867, when the Goryō Eji came to recover Itō's body at Aburanokōji and fell into the Shinsengumi's ambush, Shinohara was among those who fought their way out, escaping to the Satsuma domain's residence. The survivors did not forget: a few weeks later Kondō was ambushed and shot through the shoulder near Fushimi, and Shinohara is named among the men responsible. In the Boshin War he fought on the new government's side.
In the Meiji era he took the name Hata Shigechika and served in minor official posts. He died in 1911, aged about eighty-three. His diary and recollections — a rare view of the Shinsengumi from Itō's side — remain an important source for historians.
**Character** Shinohara was older than most of the corps, worldly, practical and tough — a man who had made his own way from the bottom of Kyushu society to the inner circle of an Edo scholar. He was the sort of lieutenant every leader wants: dependable in a fight, discreet, and loyal. He was also unforgiving. The man who fought his way out of Aburanokōji is named among those who shot Kondō weeks later, and in old age his recollections still burn with the grievance of that night.
**Comrades** Itō Kashitarō was the centre of his loyalties: Shinohara followed him into the corps, out of it, and avenged him. Suzuki Mikisaburō and Hattori Takeo were his fellows in Itō's party; Hattori died at Aburanokōji, and Shinohara and Suzuki escaped together. Kondō, the man he blamed for Itō's death, was the target of the ambush at Sumizome. In the Meiji era, as Hata Shigechika, he left the diary and recollections that are now one of the few views of the Shinsengumi from Itō's side.

### Hattori Takeo [p:hattori]
Role: Goryō Eji · Life: 1832–1867
A powerful two-sword fighter from Akō who joined the corps, followed Itō into the Goryō Eji, and died at Aburanokōji fighting alone against many — a last stand that even his enemies remembered with respect.
**Origins** Hattori was born in 1832 in the Akō domain of Harima province, famous throughout Japan as the home of the Forty-Seven Rōnin. He was a big, strong man and a skilled swordsman who fought with two swords at once — a rare style that demanded both strength and long training. He joined the Shinsengumi in its Kyoto years and served as an inspector, and he was close to Itō Kashitarō's circle.
**Character** Hattori was a big, powerful man, a skilled swordsman who fought with two swords at once — a rare and demanding style — and by every account brave to the point of recklessness. He came from Akō, the domain whose forty-seven retainers had avenged their lord in 1703 and become Japan's great example of loyalty to the death, and his own last fight looks like something from that story. Little else of his character survives, except the admiration of the men who killed him.
**The last stand** In 1867 he left with Itō to join the Goryō Eji. On the night of December 13, 1867, he was among the men who went to Aburanokōji to recover Itō's body. When the Shinsengumi sprang its ambush, Hattori stood his ground with a sword in each hand while those around him fell or fled. He was overwhelmed in the end; his body was found covered in wounds.
Shinsengumi survivors, Nagakura Shinpachi among them, later spoke of his courage that night with open admiration. He was thirty-five, and he lies with Itō and Tōdō at Kaikō-ji.
**Comrades** Hattori served in the corps as an inspector, and was close to Itō Kashitarō's circle from early on. He went with Itō into the Goryō Eji alongside Tōdō Heisuke, Shinohara Taishinosuke and Suzuki Mikisaburō, and on the night of Aburanokōji he stood his ground while Shinohara and Suzuki got away. Nagakura Shinpachi, who fought against him that night, later spoke of his courage with open admiration. He lies with Itō and Tōdō at Kaikō-ji.
Legend & disputed: The details of Hattori's last fight — how many he faced, how long he held out — come mainly from Shinsengumi survivors' later recollections, which may have grown in the telling. That he fought hard and died with many wounds is consistently reported.

### Yamazaki Susumu [p:yamazaki]
Role: Inspector (kansatsu) · Life: ?–1868
The corps' eyes and ears: an Osaka man who became its chief of intelligence, credited with the surveillance that led to the Ikedaya raid, and who later learned field medicine. Wounded in the retreat from Toba–Fushimi, he died on the way to Edo.
**An Osaka man** Yamazaki came from Osaka; by tradition he was the son of an acupuncturist, and his birth year is uncertain. He joined the Shinsengumi in its first Kyoto year, one of the recruits drawn from the Kansai region rather than from Tama, and his knowledge of the merchant cities of Osaka and Kyoto proved more valuable than any sword.
**The inspector** By 1864 he was serving as an inspector and investigator (shoshi shirabeyaku ken kansatsu) — the officers who watched the corps' own members and gathered intelligence in the city. In the weeks before the Ikedaya incident, Yamazaki and other investigators are credited with the surveillance of the merchant Furutaka Shuntarō, whose arrest and confession revealed the loyalist plot. The corps' most famous night began with his quiet work.
The inspectors were the least visible and most feared officers in the corps. They reported directly to the leadership, and their findings could mean expulsion or death for a member who broke the code. Yamazaki held the post for the rest of the corps' time in Kyoto.
**The corps' doctor** When the shogunate's physician Matsumoto Ryōjun visited the corps in 1865 to inspect its health, he found the barracks full of sick men and set about reforming its hygiene. Yamazaki is said to have studied under him, learning the dressing of wounds and basic surgery, and to have served thereafter as the corps' medic — a man whom Ryōjun reportedly called the Shinsengumi's own doctor.
**Death** In the fighting at Toba–Fushimi in January 1868, Yamazaki was badly wounded during the retreat south of Kyoto. He was taken aboard the shogunate's ships that carried the defeated army from Osaka back to Edo, and died on the voyage or shortly after arriving; by the most common account he was buried at sea.
**Character** Yamazaki was the corps' quiet professional. A man of Osaka among the farmers' sons of Tama, he knew the merchant cities, their dialects and their ways, and he was patient, observant and discreet — the qualities of a good investigator. He was also willing to learn something new in mid-career, and took up field medicine when the corps needed it. Little survives in his own words; his character is known from what the corps trusted him to do.
**Comrades** As an inspector Yamazaki answered to the leadership, and above all to Hijikata, who ran the corps' intelligence and discipline. His most famous work was the surveillance of the merchant Furutaka Shuntarō, carried out with other investigators, that led to the Ikedaya. Matsumoto Ryōjun, the shogunate's physician, was his teacher in medicine and by tradition called him the corps' own doctor. He belonged, with Matsubara Chūji and the Tani brothers, to the Kansai men who gave the Tama-born corps its knowledge of the western cities. He died among his comrades on the ships that carried them home from defeat.
Legend & disputed: Much of Yamazaki's popular image — the master spy in disguise, the acupuncturist's son, the burial at sea — rests on later tradition, and the exact place and date of his death are disputed. His role in the Furutaka surveillance is widely credited but the surviving records name several investigators.

### Yoshimura Kan'ichirō [p:yoshimura]
Role: Inspector; sword instructor · Life: ?–1868
A swordsman from the Nanbu domain of Morioka who joined in 1865 and rose to inspector and sword instructor. He vanished from the record after Toba–Fushimi; a century and a half later, a bestselling novel turned him into the most loved figure in the Shinsengumi story.
**From Nanbu** Yoshimura came from the Nanbu domain of Morioka, in the far north of Honshu, where he is generally said to have been a low-ranking retainer. Nanbu was large on the map and poor in fact: cold, mountainous, repeatedly struck by famine, and far from the politics of Kyoto. A low-ranking man of the north had few prospects at home. He left the domain and joined the Shinsengumi in 1865, in one of the recruiting drives that brought new men from the east after the Ikedaya made the corps famous.
**In the corps** His skill with the sword quickly earned him a place among the corps' instructors in swordsmanship, and he served as an inspector and investigator. These appointments place him among the trusted middle officers of the corps' later Kyoto years.
**Disappearance** After the battle of Toba–Fushimi in January 1868, Yoshimura disappears from the record. He may have been killed in the fighting or in the retreat; no reliable account of his end survives.
**Character and comrades** Almost nothing of Yoshimura's character or friendships survives in the record. That he was made an instructor in swordsmanship says he was a fine swordsman; that he was made an inspector says the leadership trusted his judgment and discretion. Beyond that, the man is unknown — which is precisely why a novelist could later give him a whole inner life, a family left behind in the north and a death of his own choosing.
Legend & disputed: Yoshimura is best known today through Asada Jirō's novel Mibu Gishiden (2000) and its film and television adaptations: a poor samurai who left his family to earn money for them, and who died by seppuku at the Nanbu domain's Osaka residence after Toba–Fushimi. That story is fiction, built on the handful of facts above. It has shaped how many readers picture the whole corps.

### Ogata Shuntarō [p:ogata]
Role: Inspector; instructor in letters · Life: ?–?
A learned man from Higo (Kumamoto) who served the corps from its early Kyoto years as an officer, inspector and teacher of letters, travelled with Kondō on the shogunate's missions to Hiroshima, and followed the corps as far as Aizu — where he slips out of the record.
**A scholar among swordsmen** Ogata came from Higo province in Kyushu, the domain of the Hosokawa lords of Kumamoto, which prided itself on its school and its tradition of learning. He joined the Shinsengumi early in its Kyoto period and was valued less for his sword than for his learning: he served as an assistant vice-commander, as an inspector and investigator, and as one of the corps' instructors in letters — the men who could read, write and argue in the formal language of politics.
**Close to Kondō** In 1865 and 1866 he accompanied Kondō and Itō on the shogunate's missions to Hiroshima to question envoys of Chōshū, a sign of how far Kondō trusted him. He remained with the corps through the fall of the shogunate, the retreat to Edo and the march north.
**Into silence** Ogata is last clearly recorded with the corps in Aizu in 1868. What happened to him afterwards is not known with certainty.
**Character and comrades** Ogata comes through the record as a discreet, capable man whom Kondō trusted with work that needed a pen rather than a sword. On the Hiroshima missions he travelled with Kondō and Itō Kashitarō, the corps' other great scholar, and like Takeda Kanryūsai he was one of the few officers who could match the political men of the age in argument. Unlike Itō, he never broke with Kondō, and unlike Takeda, he never seems to have made enemies. He followed the corps through defeat and into Aizu, and then left no trace of what he thought of any of it.
Legend & disputed: Some researchers have argued that Ogata returned to Kumamoto and lived on quietly under another name into the Meiji era. The theory is interesting but not proven, and the absence of any account from him — for a man so close to Kondō — is itself one of the small mysteries of the corps.

### Shimada Kai [p:shimada]
Role: Sergeant (gochō); officer at Hakodate · Life: 1828–1900
A giant of a man who joined in 1863, served as a sergeant through the Kyoto years, followed Hijikata all the way to Hakodate, and survived. He spent his last years as a guard at Nishi Honganji — the corps' old headquarters — and his diary is one of the most important records of the Shinsengumi.
**Origins** Shimada was born in 1828 in Mino province, in what is now Gifu prefecture. He grew into an exceptionally large and strong man — the sort others remembered first by his size — and trained as a swordsman in Edo, where he came to know Nagakura Shinpachi. In 1863 he joined the newly formed corps in Kyoto.
**The sergeant** Through the Kyoto years Shimada served as a sergeant (gochō), one of the non-commissioned officers who led small squads on patrol under the unit captains. He took part in the corps' surveillance of the loyalist underground and in its major actions. He was steady, loyal and well liked — never one of the famous names, but one of the men the corps ran on.
**To the end** When the shogunate fell, Shimada stayed. He fought at Toba–Fushimi, at Katsunuma, in the north, and finally at Hakodate, where he was one of Hijikata's closest followers and an officer of the much-reduced corps. He surrendered with the corps at Benten Daiba in 1869 and was held as a prisoner.
**Character** Shimada was remembered first for his size — a giant by the standards of his day — and then for his steadiness. He was not a schemer, a talker or a leader of factions; he was the kind of non-commissioned officer every unit depends on, who did what he was asked, stayed when others left, and kept a written record of it all. In later life he was quiet, devout and loyal to the memory of the dead.
**Comrades** Shimada knew Nagakura Shinpachi from Edo — by tradition they had trained at the same dōjō before either went west — and the two stayed close; in 1864 Shimada was one of the six who signed the protest against Kondō's arrogance. He followed Hijikata from Kyoto to Hakodate and was among his closest men in the last campaign. At Benten Daiba he surrendered with Sōma Kazue, the corps' last commander. His diary, kept from Kyoto to Hakodate, is the record on which much of every later history rests.
**Keeper of memory** After his release Shimada settled in Kyoto and found work as a guard at Nishi Honganji, the great temple where the corps had once kept its headquarters. He kept the memory of his dead comrades for the rest of his life. He died in 1900, aged seventy-two.
The diary he kept — covering the corps' movements from Kyoto to Hakodate — is one of the core sources on which every history of the Shinsengumi depends.
Legend & disputed: Popular stories make Shimada a gentle giant with a sweet tooth, or have him hauling Nagakura over a wall in a single heave. They suit his size and his reputation, but they come from later anecdote rather than record.

### Sōma Kazue [p:soma]
Role: Last commander of the Shinsengumi · Life: 1843–1875
A Kasama man who joined late, stood beside Kondō at Nagareyama when Kondō gave himself up, fought on to Hakodate, and became the last commander of the Shinsengumi in its final days — signing its surrender. Exiled, pardoned, he took his own life in 1875.
**A late recruit** Sōma was born in 1843 in the Kasama domain of Hitachi province, a small domain north-east of Edo, as the son of one of its retainers. He joined the Shinsengumi late, in its last Kyoto period, and so missed the corps' famous early years — but he would see its very end.
**Nagareyama** In April 1868, when the new government's army surrounded the corps at Nagareyama and Kondō decided to give himself up under an assumed name, Sōma was one of the few men who went with him to the enemy camp. He was detained for a time; Kondō was recognised and executed.
**The last commander** Sōma made his way north and joined the corps again at Hakodate. In the last days of the campaign in May 1869, with Hijikata dead and the corps' remaining officers falling or captured, he became its commander. When the defenders of Benten Daiba gave up, it fell to him to surrender the Shinsengumi.
**After** He was sentenced to exile on the island of Niijima. Pardoned in 1872, he served briefly as an official, then in 1875 took his own life. Why is not known.
**Character and comrades** What little is known of Sōma suggests a conscientious, educated man who took responsibility when others would not. At Nagareyama he went with Kondō into the enemy camp, together with Nomura Risaburō; both were held for a time and released, and Nomura was killed a year later in the raid on Miyako Bay. At Hakodate he served under Hijikata, and in the last days, with Hijikata dead, he took command of the remnant of the corps and surrendered it at Benten Daiba alongside Shimada Kai. On Niijima, by island tradition, he taught the children and married a local woman. Then, in 1875, he ended his own life, and left no explanation.
Legend & disputed: Sōma's exact title and the date he took command at Hakodate vary between sources, and the reasons for his suicide have attracted speculation — from disgrace at work to the weight of being the man who surrendered the corps — none of it documented.

### Mori Tsunekichi [p:mori]
Role: Chief officer (tōdori) of the corps at Hakodate · Life: 1826–1869
A senior retainer of the Kuwana domain who followed his lord to Hakodate, took command of the Shinsengumi there as its chief officer, and after the surrender accepted responsibility for Kuwana's part in the war — dying for it in 1869.
**A Kuwana retainer** Mori was a senior retainer of the Kuwana domain, whose lord Matsudaira Sadaaki was Katamori's younger brother and had served as the shogunate's governor of Kyoto — the corps' other great patron. When Sadaaki fled north rather than submit, Mori and a band of Kuwana men went with him, all the way to Hakodate.
**Chief officer** At Hakodate the Kuwana men were enrolled in the Shinsengumi, and Mori became one of its chief officers (tōdori). A man of rank and age, he gave the corps — by then a mix of Kyoto veterans and newcomers from the north — a commander of standing in the last campaign.
**Responsibility** After the surrender in 1869, the new government demanded that each rebel domain name the men responsible. Mori accepted that role for Kuwana. In the winter of 1869 he was put to death — by most accounts ordered to commit seppuku — so that his domain could be pardoned. He is remembered in Kuwana for that sacrifice.
**Character and comrades** Mori belonged to a different world from the Kyoto veterans: an older man of rank in an old domain, used to dealing with lords and officials. Kuwana sat on Ise Bay on the Tōkaidō, and its lord Matsudaira Sadaaki was the younger brother of Aizu's Katamori and had served the shogunate as governor of Kyoto in the corps' years there; Kuwana men had worked alongside the Shinsengumi in the city.
His loyalty was to his lord first. He followed Sadaaki north to Aizu, Sendai and Hakodate, enrolled in the Shinsengumi when that was the way to stay with the army, and served under Hijikata as one of its chief officers beside Ōno Uchū of Karatsu. In the end he gave his life so that his domain could be pardoned, and he is remembered in Kuwana for that above all.
Legend & disputed: Sources differ on Mori's exact title in the corps and on the date and manner of his death; he is often described as the only man put to death for the Hakodate war, a claim worth checking against the full record.

### Ōno Uchū [p:ono]
Role: Chief officer (tōdori), then on Hijikata's staff · Life: 1836–1911
A Karatsu retainer who came north with his domain's heir, joined the Shinsengumi as a chief officer, and served on Hijikata's staff in Hakodate. He was with Hijikata on his last day, and his account is one of the main sources for how Hijikata died.
**From Karatsu** Ōno was a retainer of the Karatsu domain in northern Kyushu. His domain's heir, Ogasawara Nagamichi, had been one of the shogunate's senior councillors and refused to submit to the new government; Ōno followed him north into the war.
**With Hijikata** In the north he joined the Shinsengumi as one of its chief officers (tōdori), and at Hakodate he served under Hijikata, who had become one of the army's senior commanders. On the last day of Hijikata's life, May 11 by the old calendar (June 20, 1869), Ōno was in the fighting with him near Ippongi. He survived the war.
**The witness** After the war Ōno served in the Meiji government and lived until 1911. His written account of the Hakodate campaign records Hijikata's final hours from close by, and every modern telling of Hijikata's death draws on it.
**Character and comrades** Ōno was an educated, capable officer, which is why Hijikata made him his adjutant in Ezo and why, after the war, the Meiji government found a use for him. Like Mori Tsunekichi, he came to the Shinsengumi not from the Kyoto years but from loyalty to a lord: Ogasawara Nagamichi, heir to Karatsu and a former senior councillor of the shogunate, who went north and on to Hakodate rather than submit. In the north he met the men of the old corps, and in Hijikata a commander he admired. His account of the war, and of Hijikata's last day, is the monument he left to them.
Legend & disputed: Ōno's account is invaluable but not the only one, and the various witnesses disagree on details of Hijikata's death — where exactly he fell and what he said. Ōno's title and duties also differ between sources.

### Niimi Nishiki [p:niimi]
Role: Commander, Serizawa faction · Life: 1836–1863
Serizawa Kamo's chief lieutenant: a Mito swordsman who briefly shared command of the corps with Serizawa and Kondō, was demoted for misconduct, and was forced to commit seppuku in a Gion teahouse in the autumn of 1863 — the first blow of the purge that destroyed his faction.
**Serizawa's man** Niimi came, like Serizawa, from the Mito region and its radical loyalist circles, and was a skilled swordsman of Shintō Munen-ryū. He travelled to Kyoto with Serizawa in the Rōshigumi and stayed with him when the rest returned east. His real name and early life are uncertain.
**Three commanders** In the corps' first months its command was shared among three commanders: Serizawa, Kondō and Niimi. It was an uneasy arrangement between two factions with very different habits. Niimi was soon demoted to vice-commander, and the Serizawa faction's extortion and violence in the city were steadily turning the corps' Aizu patrons against them.
**Death in Gion** In the autumn of 1863 Niimi was confronted at a teahouse in Gion with charges of misconduct and forced to commit seppuku. A few days later Serizawa himself was killed at the Yagi house in Mibu. Niimi's death removed Serizawa's strongest supporter first — whether by design or not, it cleared the way for everything that followed.
**Character and comrades** Little of Niimi's own character survives; he is known almost entirely through Serizawa and through the faction's misconduct, which he is said to have shared — drinking, extortion and violence in the city. He was a fellow Mito man and Shintō Munen-ryū swordsman, Serizawa's right hand, and with Hirayama Gorō, Hirama Jūsuke and Noguchi Kenji part of the small band that came west with him.
His relations with Kondō's group were those of a rival faction: for a few months he shared the command with Kondō and Serizawa, and he was the first of the Serizawa men to be removed. Who confronted him in the Gion teahouse is not certain; later accounts name members of Kondō's side.
Legend & disputed: Niimi's identity has been debated; some researchers identify him with a Mito man named Niinomi Kumetarō. The exact charges against him, and who pressed them, come mostly from accounts by the winning side.

### Matsudaira Katamori [p:katamori]
Role: Lord of Aizu; Protector of Kyoto · Life: 1836–1893
The lord of Aizu who accepted the thankless post of Protector of Kyoto in 1862, took the Shinsengumi under his authority, and won the deep trust of Emperor Kōmei — only to be branded a rebel after the emperor's death, besieged in his own castle, and defeated. He outlived the corps by a quarter of a century.
**A reluctant guardian** Katamori was born in Edo in 1836, a son of the lord of Takasu, a small branch domain of the great Tokugawa house of Owari. He was one of four brothers who all rose high: Tokugawa Yoshikatsu became lord of Owari, Mochiharu head of the Hitotsubashi house, and the youngest, Matsudaira Sadaaki, lord of Kuwana. Katamori himself was adopted into the ruling house of Aizu as a boy and inherited it in 1852. Aizu was bound to the shogunate by the precepts of its founder, Hoshina Masayuki, who had charged his descendants to serve the Tokugawa above all.
In 1862 the shogunate created a new post — Protector of Kyoto (Kyōto shugoshoku) — to restore order in a capital overrun by loyalist assassins, and pressed it on Katamori. His senior retainers warned him that it would ruin the domain. He accepted anyway, out of duty, and in early 1863 entered Kyoto with a thousand Aizu troops.
**Patron of the Shinsengumi** When the Rōshigumi fell apart in the spring of 1863, the men who stayed behind in Kyoto under Kondō and Serizawa were placed under Aizu's authority. It was Aizu that gave the corps its legitimacy, its pay and, after the coup of August 18, 1863, its name: Shinsengumi. Katamori's men demanded order of their new auxiliaries, and Aizu's patience with Serizawa's outrages ran out that autumn.
From then on the corps was Aizu's instrument in the streets of Kyoto: it reported to Aizu, fought beside Aizu at the Ikedaya and in the Kinmon incident, and rose with Aizu's standing.
**The emperor's trust** Emperor Kōmei, who distrusted the radicals of Chōshū, came to rely on Katamori more than on any other lord. In 1863 he sent him a personal letter of thanks and a poem — an extraordinary mark of favour that Katamori kept close for the rest of his life. When Chōshū troops attacked the palace in the Kinmon incident of 1864, Katamori's Aizu forces held the gates.
**Defeat and survival** Kōmei's sudden death in early 1867 removed Katamori's protector at court. Within a year the shogunate had fallen, and Aizu — the domain that had policed Kyoto for the Tokugawa — was declared an enemy of the court. In the autumn of 1868 the new government's armies besieged Tsuruga castle for a month; the town burned, and the youths of the Byakkotai and many others died. Katamori surrendered in November 1868.
He was spared. After years of confinement he became chief priest of the Tōshō-gū shrine at Nikkō, dedicated to the first Tokugawa shogun, and died in 1893. The emperor's letter was found among his belongings.
**Character** Katamori was earnest, dutiful and often in poor health — a man who took on burdens because he believed it was his place to, and then carried them to the end. He was reserved, inclined to say little, and inspired an intense loyalty in his retainers, many of whom died for him in 1868. His tragedy was to be completely loyal to two masters, the shogun and the emperor, at the moment when they parted.
**Relationships** To the Shinsengumi he was patron and protector: it was Katamori who took the men of Mibu under his wing in 1863, reconciled Kondō with his protesting comrades in 1864, and, twenty-five years later, wrote the title on the monument to Kondō and Hijikata at Takahata. Kondō served him with a devotion he returned.
Emperor Kōmei trusted him above all other lords. His brother Sadaaki of Kuwana stood beside him in Kyoto as governor of the city and fought beside him in the war. With the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, the relationship ended badly: when Yoshinobu abandoned the army at Osaka in January 1868, he took Katamori with him on his flight to Edo, leaving Aizu's men behind — a humiliation that Aizu did not forget. In later life Aizu's people remained his circle, and among them was Saitō Hajime, at whose marriage he acted as go-between.

### Kiyokawa Hachirō [p:kiyokawa]
Role: Founder of the Rōshigumi · Life: 1830–1863
The brilliant, restless schemer who talked the shogunate into recruiting masterless swordsmen to guard Kyoto — and then, the moment they arrived, tried to turn them against it. The men who refused to follow him became the Shinsengumi. He was assassinated in Edo weeks later.
**Scholar and agitator** Kiyokawa was born in 1830 in the Shōnai region of Dewa province, the son of a wealthy sake-brewing family. He went to Edo to study, mastered both Confucian learning and Hokushin Ittō-ryū swordsmanship, and opened a school that taught both. By 1860 he was at the centre of a circle of young men dedicated to expelling the foreigners, among them Yamaoka Tesshū.
In 1861 he killed a man who had provoked him in the street and became a fugitive, travelling the country and building connections with radical loyalists in Kyushu and Kyoto.
**The Rōshigumi** In 1862 Kiyokawa proposed a bold idea to the shogunate through sympathetic officials: pardon the masterless samurai and swordsmen roaming the country and recruit them as a corps to guard the shogun on his coming visit to Kyoto. The shogunate, short of reliable men and alarmed by the chaos in the capital, agreed. In early 1863 some 230 recruits — Kondō Isami and his Shieikan circle and Serizawa Kamo's Mito group among them — set out along the Nakasendō.
**The turn** The day after the Rōshigumi reached Kyoto and settled in Mibu, Kiyokawa revealed his real aim: the corps would serve the emperor and the cause of expelling the foreigners, not the shogunate. He had a petition to that effect submitted to the court. Alarmed, the shogunate recalled the Rōshigumi to Edo.
Most of the men went back with him. But a small group refused — Kondō's men and Serizawa's — insisting that they had come to guard the shogun and would stay to do so. Taken under the authority of Aizu, they became the Shinsengumi. Kiyokawa's plan had created the very corps that would spend the next five years hunting men like him.
**Assassination** Back in Edo, Kiyokawa was assassinated on May 30, 1863 (the 4th month by the old calendar) near the Ichinohashi bridge in Azabu, cut down by a group of shogunal swordsmen. He was thirty-two. The men he had led back to Edo were reorganised as the Shinchōgumi, a police force under the Shōnai domain — his own home province.
**Character** Kiyokawa was brilliant, eloquent, restless and supremely sure of himself. He could talk his way into the shogunate's confidence and out of it again within weeks, and he seems never to have doubted that history would follow his plans. Admirers saw a visionary loyalist; the men he outmanoeuvred saw a reckless opportunist who spent other men's lives and money. He was also capable of real attachment: his wife Oren, a former courtesan, was arrested after his flight in 1861 and died in prison, and he grieved for her deeply.
**Relationships** Yamaoka Tesshū, the swordsman and later Meiji statesman, was his close friend and stood beside him in the Rōshigumi scheme. The shogunate's officials who backed his plan felt betrayed when he turned it against them, and it was a group of shogunal swordsmen, generally said to include Sasaki Tadasaburō, who killed him.
With Kondō Isami and Serizawa Kamo his relationship was brief and decisive: they were recruits in the corps he had created, and they refused to follow him when he revealed his aim. Their refusal founded the Shinsengumi. The men who did follow him back to Edo became the Shinchōgumi, and among them was Okita Rintarō, the brother-in-law of Okita Sōji.
Legend & disputed: Kiyokawa remains hard to judge: a principled loyalist to some, an opportunist who used the shogunate's money to raise a force for its enemies to others. The traditional story that he announced his plan in a dramatic speech to the assembled men at Mibu's Shintoku-ji is well established in outline, though accounts of what he said differ.

## The story page

### Farmers who wanted to be samurai
The Shinsengumi began not in a castle but in the farm villages of Tama, west of Edo. Its two central figures, Kondō Isami and Hijikata Toshizō, were both sons of well-off farming families. Tama was shogunal land, governed directly by the Tokugawa, and its farmers were proud of that bond: they read, wrote poetry, and many trained with the sword.
Their school was the Tennen Rishin-ryū, a rough, practical style that Edo’s fashionable dojos looked down on. Its Edo hall, the Shieikan in Ichigaya, was run by Kondō and was less a school than a household. The teenage prodigy Okita Sōji lived there, and guests such as Nagakura Shinpachi, Harada Sanosuke and the scholarly Yamanami Keisuke ate at the same table. In the rigid class order of the day, none of them could become samurai by birth. What they wanted was to become samurai by deed.

### A country in crisis
In 1853 Commodore Perry’s warships forced open a Japan that had kept itself closed for more than two centuries. The shogunate’s inability to resist them shook its authority to the core. A movement called sonnō jōi, “revere the emperor, expel the barbarians,” spread among young samurai, and its stage became Kyoto, the imperial capital.
By the early 1860s radical activists, the shishi, were assassinating officials and their supporters in Kyoto’s streets and putting their heads on display as “heaven’s punishment.” The shogunate needed men who could keep order there.

### From the Rōshigumi to the Shinsengumi
In early 1863 the shogunate recruited masterless swordsmen into the Rōshigumi to guard the shogun Tokugawa Iemochi on his visit to Kyoto. Kondō and his Shieikan circle joined. Once in Kyoto, the scheme’s organiser, Kiyokawa Hachirō, announced that he meant to put the men at the service of the imperial loyalist cause, and the force was recalled to Edo.
About two dozen men refused to go back: Kondō’s group and the followers of Serizawa Kamo from Mito. They petitioned Matsudaira Katamori, lord of Aizu and the shogunate’s Military Commissioner of Kyoto, and were taken on under his authority. Quartered in the farming village of Mibu, they were mocked in town as the “wolves of Mibu.” They stood guard at the palace during the coup of August 18, 1863, which drove the radical Chōshū faction from the court, and around this time received the name they are remembered by: Shinsengumi, the “newly selected corps.”
Two things set them apart. One was their look: light-blue haori jackets with a white zigzag (dandara) pattern at the sleeve ends, and a banner bearing a single character, 誠, makoto, sincerity. The other was their code. It forbade straying from the way of the samurai, deserting the corps, raising money privately, taking up private lawsuits and fighting private quarrels, on pain of seppuku (its exact wording survives only in later accounts). The code was enforced. That autumn Serizawa, whose drunken violence and extortion had become a liability, was killed in a night attack on his quarters, and Kondō as commander, with Hijikata as vice-commander, took sole control.

### Guardians of Kyoto
The Shinsengumi’s job was policing. Under Aizu’s command its units patrolled the streets, searched inns, and arrested or cut down those they judged to be plotters. At its height the corps is said to have numbered some two hundred men.
Its defining night came on July 8, 1864. Under torture, an arrested merchant, Furutaka Shuntarō, revealed a plan by radicals to set fire to Kyoto on a windy night around the Gion festival and, according to his confession, carry the emperor off to Chōshū in the confusion. Kondō led a handful of men through the front door of the Ikedaya inn while Hijikata’s column closed off the streets. Several leading activists were killed or captured. The plot may have been exaggerated, but the raid made the Shinsengumi famous overnight. It is often said to have delayed the Meiji Restoration by a year, though that is hard to prove.
Weeks later, enraged Chōshū troops marched on the palace in the Kinmon incident, and the Shinsengumi fought alongside Aizu and Satsuma to drive them back. In 1867, in recognition of its service, the corps’ members were made direct retainers of the shogunate. The farmers’ sons from Tama had become samurai in status as well as in spirit.

### Cracks, and the end of the shogunate
Discipline came at a cost. In 1865 Yamanami Keisuke, one of the corps’ founding leaders, left without permission. Brought back, he was ordered to commit seppuku. By many counts, more members died at the hands of their own corps than in battle with the enemy.
In 1867 a faction led by Itō Kashitarō broke away, and on December 13 Itō was ambushed and killed at Aburanokōji. Tōdō Heisuke, one of the original Shieikan circle, died in the fight that followed. By then the world the corps served was coming apart. In November 1867 the shogun Tokugawa Yoshinobu returned political authority to the emperor, and in January 1868 a new imperial government was proclaimed that left no place for the Tokugawa.

### The Boshin War
The war that ended the shogunate began at Toba-Fushimi, south of Kyoto, on January 27, 1868. Swords met modern rifles and artillery, and the shogunate’s forces were routed. Afterwards Hijikata is said to have told an acquaintance that swords and spears were no longer of any use in war.
The corps fell back to Edo. Renamed the Kōyō Chinbutai, it was beaten at Kōshū-Katsunuma in March. Afterwards Nagakura Shinpachi and Harada Sanosuke, who refused to become Kondō’s retainers, left to fight on in a unit of their own; Harada was killed at the battle of Ueno that summer. In April, at Nagareyama, Kondō gave himself up to the new government’s army under a false name, by most accounts to buy time for his men to escape. He was recognised, and on May 17, 1868, he was beheaded at Itabashi. Two months later Okita Sōji, still in his twenties, died of tuberculosis in hiding in Edo.
Hijikata fought on, north through Utsunomiya and Aizu, where Saitō Hajime stayed to fight alongside the Aizu domain. At Sendai Hijikata joined the shogunate’s fleet under Enomoto Takeaki and sailed for Ezo, today’s Hokkaido. There the holdouts took the star-shaped fortress of Goryōkaku and set up their own government, with Hijikata commanding its army. On June 20, 1869, during the new government’s assault on Hakodate, he was shot from his horse and killed, aged 34, riding to relieve the last men of the Shinsengumi, cut off at the Benten Daiba battery. Four days later they surrendered under their last commander, Sōma Kazue, with veterans such as Shimada Kai among them. Three days after that Goryōkaku surrendered, and the war was over.

### Survivors, and the legend
A few members lived long into the new era. Nagakura Shinpachi settled in Hokkaido and, late in life, told his story to a newspaper; it remains one of the few first-hand accounts we have. Saitō Hajime became a police officer in Tokyo under the name Fujita Gorō and fought in the Satsuma Rebellion for the government he had once fought against. Both died in 1915.
For decades the Meiji state remembered the Shinsengumi as rebels and killers who had taken up arms against the emperor. The tide turned in the twentieth century: Shimozawa Kan’s oral-history accounts in the 1920s, Shiba Ryōtarō’s novels Moeyo Ken and Shinsengumi Keppūroku in the 1960s, and then films, television drama, manga and games made them heroes. Much of what “everyone knows” about the Shinsengumi comes from this later fiction, which is why this site keeps legend in separate, clearly marked boxes.

### Why they still inspire
The Shinsengumi were a police force on the losing side, and their history includes assassinations, purges and a harsh code. Yet they remain among the most loved groups in Japanese history, for several reasons.
They rose by their own effort. In a society where status was fixed at birth, farmers’ sons and masterless swordsmen became samurai through skill and service.
They kept faith with a cause after it was lost. Even after the shogun himself gave up power, Hijikata and his comrades fought on for what they had sworn to, all the way to Hakodate. The single character on their banner, 誠, sincerity, sums up why that loyalty still moves people.
They were a team. Behind the legend is a household of friends from a poor Edo dojo, and the corps’ strength came from that bond as much as from its swords.
And they show the cost of absolute rules. The discipline that made the corps effective also led it to kill its own. Admiring them honestly means holding both sides.

### About this website
This site maps the story of the Shinsengumi — the men of a small Edo sword school who became the shogun’s police in Kyoto and fought on to Hakodate — together with the people around them and the national events of their age. On the map, numbered pins mark the corps’ main story in date order, and ◇ marks the great events happening around them. Tap a member’s name to follow one man through his whole life, from birth to grave: the map adds his own events (marked •), dims the rest and draws his path, and his page gives his biography in full. The guided tour walks the corps’ main story in order; choose a member first, and it follows his life instead. The people page lists every member covered so far.
It is a personal research project that grows as its author reads, and it tries to keep documented history apart from later legend: the dashed red boxes hold stories that come from fiction or disputed accounts. Dates and places come from standard secondary sources and are being checked against primary records over time. The zigzag strip under the header echoes the dandara pattern on the corps’ haori.
